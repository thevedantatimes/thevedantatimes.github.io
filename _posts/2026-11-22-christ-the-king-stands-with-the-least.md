There are kings who are remembered for the armies they led, the territories they annexed, and the monuments they left in stone. There are kings who are remembered for the courts they kept, splendid and remote, where admission depended on rank and favour and where an ordinary person might live a whole lifetime without once being seen by the sovereign. And then, on the last Sunday of the Church's year, Christians are asked to contemplate a King who fits neither picture at all.

The Solemnity of Our Lord Jesus Christ, King of the Universe, closes the liturgical year. Next Sunday a new year will begin with Advent, with candles and waiting and a child promised in the dark of winter. But before that beginning, the Church sets this ending. She gathers up the whole long journey through the seasons — the teaching in Galilee, the road to Jerusalem, the cross, the empty tomb, the long green weeks of ordinary time — and she asks, in effect, what it has all been about. Who, finally, is in charge of this story? And what kind of rule does he exercise?

The answer given by the feast is startling precisely because it refuses every ordinary expectation of kingship. This King's throne, in the Christian telling, is a cross. His crown is woven from thorns. His royal progress was not a procession through cheering avenues but a walk, under guard, to a place of execution outside the city wall. His court, if the Gospel for this day is to be believed, is not found among the powerful at all. It is found among the hungry and the thirsty, the stranger at the gate, the naked, the sick, and the prisoner behind a locked door.

That is not a metaphor the Church adds to soften a harder doctrine. It is the doctrine itself, spoken by the King in his own final teaching before the Passion. In the Gospel appointed for this solemnity, Matthew 25:31-46, the Son of Man comes in glory, sits on the throne of his glory, and gathers all the nations before him. And the judgment that follows does not turn on what most human courts examine. It does not turn on wealth accumulated, honours won, or even, in any simple sense, on religious profession declared aloud. It turns on whether a person fed someone who was hungry. Whether they gave drink to someone who was thirsty. Whether they welcomed, clothed, visited, and came near.

For readers of The Vedanta Times, who come to the Christian Sunday from many directions, this feast offers an honest place to begin. It does not ask for agreement about every article of Christian doctrine before it will speak. It asks a simpler and more searching question, one every great tradition knows in its own idiom: by what measure will a life finally be weighed? By what we kept, or by what we gave? By how high we rose, or by how low we stooped to meet another human being in need?

The paradox at the heart of the day can be stated quite plainly. The Christian claim is that the ruler of the universe is most truly himself not when he is distant, but when he is near. Not when he is served, but when he is found, disguised and unrecognised, in the person who needs serving. The King stands with the least. That phrase — the least — will return again and again in what follows, because Jesus himself returns to it. The least of these. The smallest, the overlooked, the ones a busy and successful world has learned not to see.

To understand why the Church ends her year here, it helps to remember what a liturgical year is for. It is not simply a calendar of commemorations. It is a school. Week by week it trains attention — teaching a community what to notice, what to value, what to grieve, and what to hope for. A year that ends with a King identifying himself with the hungry suggests that the whole preceding year — every parable, every healing, every meal shared with outcasts — was leading to this unveiling of what divine rule actually looks like.

None of this makes the feast sentimental. Matthew 25 is among the most solemn passages in the New Testament, and the Church does not read it on this day to produce a warm feeling. It includes separation. It includes words of welcome and words of dismissal. It speaks of an eternal horizon. The mercy it celebrates is not vague kindness; it is concrete, costly, and, in the scene Jesus describes, decisive. Part of the work of this article is to read that passage slowly, without hurrying past either its comfort or its warning.

But first the day itself has spoken, in our own time, in a particular place. On this very Sunday, in St Peter's Square in Rome, the present successor of Peter stood before the crowds at the midday Angelus and reflected on this same Gospel. What he said there, and what he did afterwards — sitting down to lunch not with diplomats but with more than a thousand poor people — gives this ancient feast a present-tense doorway. The news of the day and the teaching of the day, for once, are saying the same thing.

## 1. The News of the Day: A King Announced in St Peter's Square

On Sunday, 22 November 2026, Pope Leo XIV led the Angelus prayer in St Peter's Square for the Solemnity of Our Lord Jesus Christ, King of the Universe. The text of his reflection is published by the Holy See on the Vatican website, with the date printed on the page itself: [Sunday, 22 November 2026](http://www.vatican.va/content/leo-xiv/en/angelus/2026/documents/20261122-angelus.html). Before the Angelus he had celebrated Mass for the feast, and his midday reflection turned, as the day itself turns, to the Gospel of Matthew 25.

His central claim can be given in his own verified words: "Christ is the measure of justice because he is the author of salvation". That ordering matters, and the Pope drew attention to it. Justice, in this account, is not measured first by punishment, or by the settling of scores, or by the power to compel. It is measured by Christ because salvation comes from him — because what God is doing in the world, before it is anything else, is rescuing, healing, and making whole. A judge whose deepest purpose is to save will judge differently from a judge whose deepest purpose is to condemn.

From that foundation, the reflection drew a portrait of kingship set against the kingships of this world. This King, the Pope taught, is neither a despot who threatens nor a tyrant who crushes others with his power. Rule by fear and humiliation is real, and history knows it well. The kingship proclaimed on this feast works in the opposite direction. Its strength is shown in nearness, its authority in service, its glory on a cross.

Where, then, is this King to be found? The Angelus answer followed Matthew's answer. The King stands alongside the least and the oppressed. He has, by his own declaration in the Gospel, placed himself among those the world counts last: the one without food, the one without welcome, the one without visitors. To seek the King is therefore not primarily to look upward in awe, though awe has its place. It is to look outward, and often downward in the world's estimation, in attention.

The Pope also recalled why this feast exists at all. The Solemnity of Christ the King was instituted by Pope Pius XI, and Leo XIV recalled his predecessor's intention: that the feast should serve peace and justice. It was established in a century that had seen empires collapse and violent ideologies rise to claim total allegiance. Against every earthly power that demands to be worshipped, the feast makes a counter-claim: there is one King, and his kingdom is marked not by the peace of submission imposed by force, but by the peace that grows where justice is done and the least are lifted up.

Perhaps the most striking note was its treatment of judgment itself. In popular imagination, judgment is the terrifying part of Matthew 25 — the separation, the closed door. The Angelus read the passage, instead, in the light of salvation. Judgment belongs inside the saving purpose. The King who judges is the same King who gave himself for those he judges. To be measured by such a judge is to be told the truth by the one whose truth-telling is itself an act of love — the truth about whether our lives made room for mercy, or passed by on the other side.

Words at a window in St Peter's Square are one thing. What happened after the Angelus gave those words a body. For the 10th World Day of the Poor — observed this year a week later than its usual date because of the Pope's recent journey to Latin America — Leo XIV joined a lunch with about 1,300 poor people in the Paul VI Hall, the great audience hall beside St Peter's Basilica. Consider the picture the day composed, without any need for embellishment. In the morning, a feast proclaiming Christ as King of the Universe. At midday, a reflection on the King who is found among the least. And then, at table, the head of the Catholic Church eating with more than a thousand people whom the city's ordinary economy counts as having nothing.

A shared table is not a photo opportunity in the logic of this feast; it is almost a sacrament of it. Matthew 25 begins with hunger and thirst, with the most bodily of needs. A lunch in which the poor are the guests of honour is the Gospel's first test translated into a seating plan. It says, before any sermon can, that in this kingdom the order of precedence is reversed. Those who are usually served last, or not at all, are served first. Those who usually eat alone eat in company. Those who are usually looked through are looked at, spoken with, and listened to.

The Angelus also carried the wider sorrows and hopes of the week into the feast's light. The Pope prayed for the victims of conflicts — for those living, and dying, under violence in the world's war zones, whose names rarely reach the square. He prayed, too, for the people of Papua New Guinea, suffering under severe drought, a reminder that hunger and thirst in Matthew 25 are not only spiritual images. In a drying land, "I was thirsty" is a literal sentence spoken by real communities, and the prayer named them as belonging inside the concern of this day, not outside it.

Finally, he greeted the young people marking diocesan World Youth Day, celebrated in dioceses around the world on this same solemnity. He pointed them toward the gathering to come in Seoul next August, and toward its theme from John's Gospel: "Take courage! I have overcome the world". It is a fitting word for the young on this feast. Courage, here, is not bravado. It is the quiet confidence that the world's loudest powers — fear, violence, contempt for the weak — do not have the final word. A crucified King has overcome the world not by out-shouting it, but by out-loving it.

So the news of the day is not an interruption of the feast. It is the feast, enacted in real time: a reflection on Matthew 25 in the morning's Gospel, a table for the poor at midday, prayers for the war-torn and the drought-stricken, and a word of courage for the young. The doorway is open. Now the teaching itself deserves to be walked through slowly, beginning where the day's own readings begin — not in Matthew at all, but centuries earlier, with a prophet in exile and a flock abandoned by its shepherds.

## 2. The Shepherd Who Would Tend His Own Sheep: Ezekiel 34

Long before there was a feast of Christ the King, there was a crisis of kingship in Israel, and a prophet who addressed it in some of the most tender and furious poetry in the Hebrew Bible. Ezekiel prophesied among the exiles in Babylon in the sixth century before Christ. Jerusalem had fallen. The temple was destroyed. The king had been carried away. And the question hanging over the whole community was the question every defeated people asks: who failed us, and is God still for us at all?

The thirty-fourth chapter of Ezekiel gives an answer in two movements, and the Church reads this chapter as the first reading on the Solemnity of Christ the King. The first movement is an indictment. The second is a promise. Together they form the pattern — the template, one might say — of what divine kingship means, the pattern that Jesus will later take up and embody.

The indictment is directed at the shepherds of Israel. In the ancient Near East, "shepherd" was a standard title for a king. It was not a rustic insult; it was the job description. A king was meant to tend the flock of the people: to protect them from predators, to lead them to pasture and water, to keep the weak from being trampled by the strong, to go after the strays. Hammurabi had called himself a shepherd. Israel's own kings inherited the image. David, the model king, had literally been taken from following the sheep.

Ezekiel's charge is that the shepherds have inverted the calling. Instead of feeding the sheep, they have fed themselves on the sheep. They have taken the milk, clothed themselves with the wool, and slaughtered the fat animals, while the flock itself went untended. The weak they did not strengthen. The sick they did not heal. The injured they did not bind up. The strayed they did not bring back. The lost they did not seek. And the flock, scattered across the mountains with no one to search for them, became food for wild animals.

It is worth pausing over the precision of that list, because it will echo through the rest of this article. Strengthen the weak. Heal the sick. Bind up the injured. Bring back the strayed. Seek the lost. These are not general sentiments about being a decent ruler. They are specific acts of care directed at specific forms of vulnerability. Ezekiel's God does not accuse the shepherds of insufficient pageantry. He accuses them of neglecting bodies: the weak, the sick, the wounded, the wandering, the lost. Already, six centuries before Matthew's Gospel, the measure of rule is being set by what is done for those who cannot do for themselves.

There is a further edge to the indictment. The shepherds ruled, Ezekiel says, with force and harshness. Bad shepherding is not only neglect; it is also domination, and the two failures belong together. A ruler who does not care for the weak will eventually rule them harshly, because people we do not care about become people we feel free to use. Neglect curdles into contempt, and contempt licenses cruelty.

Then comes the promise, and it is one of the great turning points of the Old Testament. God declares that he himself will do what the shepherds failed to do. He will search for his sheep and seek them out, as a shepherd searches for his flock on a day of clouds and thick darkness. He will rescue them from all the places to which they have been scattered. He will bring them home, feed them on the mountains, let them lie down in good grazing land. And then the list returns, now as promise rather than accusation: the lost he will seek, the strayed he will bring back, the injured he will bind up, the weak he will strengthen.

The image deserves to be felt, not just analysed. Binding up an injured animal is close, patient, unglamorous work. It requires kneeling and touching the wound. It cannot be done from a throne; a shepherd who binds a leg is on the ground, in the dirt, with his hands on the hurt. When God says he will bind up the injured, he is describing a kingship that kneels. Centuries later, Christians would see that image fulfilled almost literally in a room in Jerusalem, where their Lord knelt with a towel and washed the feet of his friends.

Ezekiel 34 does not end with tenderness alone, and honesty requires saying so. The chapter also speaks of judgment — judgment between sheep and sheep, between the rams and the he-goats. Within the flock itself there is injustice: the fat and strong sheep have pushed with flank and shoulder, butted the weak with their horns, fouled the water with their feet, and muddied what they did not drink so that others could not drink it either. God's promised shepherding therefore includes sorting this out. He will judge between the sheep that tramples and the sheep that is trampled. The pasture will be made fair.

This matters for how the whole day is understood, because it shows that judgment and mercy are not opposites in this tradition. The same God who binds up the injured also confronts the strong who foul the water. Mercy for the weak requires, sometimes, judgment on the behaviour that crushes them. A shepherd who never intervened between the bullying ram and the battered ewe would not be merciful; he would merely be absent. Ezekiel's vision holds the two together in one act of care: rescue for the scattered, healing for the injured, and a reckoning for the violence inside the flock itself.

The chapter closes its promise with covenant language — a covenant of peace, showers of blessing, a land secure from wild beasts, a people who will know that their God is with them. The political and the spiritual are not separated here: safety, rain in season, food in abundance, and the knowledge of God's presence arrive as one gift.

Christians reading this chapter on the feast of Christ the King hear it as a promise kept in an unexpected way. The shepherd God promised to send — for the chapter also speaks of a coming Davidic shepherd-prince — arrives, in the Christian confession, as Jesus of Nazareth. Jesus himself takes up the image explicitly in John's Gospel, calling himself the good shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep, who knows his own and is known by them, who has other sheep not of this fold whom he must also bring. The contrast with Ezekiel's hirelings and thieves could not be sharper. The hireling runs when the wolf comes, because the sheep are not his. The good shepherd stays, because they are.

But notice what the Ezekiel pattern does to the very idea of a king. If this chapter is the template, then kingship is measured from below. You do not assess the shepherd by interviewing the shepherd. You assess him by looking at the sheep — specifically at the worst-off sheep. Is the injured one bound up? Is the lost one being searched for? Is the water clean for everyone, or fouled by the strong? By that test, most kings of the ancient world, and not a few rulers since, would fail. And by that test, the strange King of this feast begins to come into focus.

There is one more subtlety that prepares the way for Matthew. In Ezekiel, God is the shepherd and the people are the sheep; the roles are clear. In Matthew 25, Jesus will occupy both sides of the image at once. He is the King who judges, enthroned in glory. And he is simultaneously the hungry one, the thirsty one, the stranger, the prisoner — the least of the flock, waiting at the roadside of our lives to see whether anyone will stop. The prophet's pattern is not discarded. It is deepened past all expectation: the shepherd has made the flock's neediest members his own address.

With Ezekiel's voice still in the ear — seek the lost, bind the injured, judge between sheep and sheep — the Gospel scene can now be read as it deserves: slowly, section by section, letting each part of the picture settle before moving to the next.

## 3. When the Son of Man Comes in His Glory: Reading Matthew 25:31-46 Slowly

The passage begins with a coming. When the Son of Man comes in his glory, Matthew writes, and all the angels with him, then he will sit on the throne of his glory. Every element of that sentence is doing work. "Son of Man" is Jesus' own favoured title for himself, drawn from the book of Daniel, where one like a son of man comes with the clouds of heaven and receives an everlasting kingdom. It is at once a humble title — it can mean simply a human being — and a majestic one, and Jesus holds both senses together throughout the Gospels. The one who comes in glory is the same one who had nowhere to lay his head.

The glory, too, needs to be understood in Matthew's own terms. This Gospel has already shown its readers a transfiguration, a mountain where Jesus' face shone like the sun. It has also, and more insistently, taught them that the Son of Man's glory is bound up with his suffering: he must go to Jerusalem, be handed over, be killed, and on the third day be raised. The throne of glory in Matthew 25 cannot be separated from that road. The King who sits to judge is recognisable, Christians confess, by his wounds. Glory here is not the opposite of the cross. It is the cross, seen from the far side of resurrection.

Before the throne, all the nations are gathered. The scale is universal and deliberately so. This is not a judgment of one people, one religion's membership roll, or one era. All the nations — every tribe, language, and generation — stand in the same scene, on the same ground, before the same King. Whatever else the passage means, it means that the measure applied there is not parochial. No one in the scene is judged by a private standard invented for them alone. The question put to humanity is one question, and it is put to everyone.

Then comes the separation, and Matthew reaches, naturally, for Ezekiel's world: as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats, so the Son of Man separates them, the sheep at his right hand and the goats at his left. In the hills of the Middle East this was an ordinary evening scene: sheep and goats grazed together through the day and were sorted at night, the goats needing more shelter against the cold. The image is not, in itself, sinister. Its solemnity here comes from its finality — this separation, unlike the evening sortings, is not undone the next morning — and from the words that follow it.

The King speaks first to those on his right, and his first word is an invitation, not a verdict. Come, he says, in the traditional rendering, you who are blessed by my Father; inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world. Linger over that phrase: prepared from the foundation of the world. Before any of the gathered nations had done anything at all — before the feeding or the failing, the welcoming or the turning away — the kingdom was prepared. Mercy, in this scene, is older than merit. The invitation is not the construction of a reward at the end of time; it is the opening of a home that was always intended. The blessed are not congratulated on having earned a prize. They are welcomed into an inheritance, and an inheritance, by definition, is received, not achieved.

Then the King gives his reasons, and the reasons are the heart of the passage, the part the Church has read, memorised, painted, and argued about ever since. For, he says, "I was hungry and you gave me food"; I was thirsty and you gave me drink; "I was a stranger and you welcomed me"; "I was naked and you gave me clothing"; I was sick and you took care of me; "I was in prison and you visited me". Six needs. Six responses. The list is almost aggressively unglamorous. There are no miracles in it, no great doctrines confessed, no heroic sacrifices of the kind that win statues. There is food, drink, a door opened, a garment, a visit to a sickroom, a visit to a cell.

And then the scene performs its great surprise — a surprise that only works if we let it be one. The righteous do not know. They ask, in genuine bewilderment, when any of this happened. When did we see you hungry? When did we see you a stranger, or naked, or in prison? They had not been keeping accounts. They had not recognised the King in the people they helped; some of them, presumably, had not thought of themselves as serving God at all. They had simply seen a hungry person and fed them. Their mercy was unselfconscious, and the passage treats that unselfconsciousness as a mark of its authenticity. Love that keeps a ledger of its own goodness, Jesus suggests elsewhere, has already received its reward. This love kept no ledger, and so it is free to be astonished.

The King's answer is the verse on which the entire solemnity turns. Truly I tell you, he says, in the passage's famous logic, just as you did it to one of the least of these, you did it to me. The identification is complete and unqualified. Not: you did something God noticed. Not: you earned credit with me. You did it to me. The hungry person was, in the King's own accounting, the King himself, present under the form of need. The passage uses no technical language for this. It simply states the identification as fact and lets the reader reckon with it.

The scene then turns to those on the left, and a slow reading must not hurry past them or soften what is said. The dismissal mirrors the welcome, clause by clause, and the mirror is the message: the same six needs, the same six opportunities, now in the negative. You gave me no food. You gave me no drink. You did not welcome, did not clothe, did not visit. And these, too, ask the bewildered question — when did we see you in need and not help? Their bewilderment is the darker twin of the righteous' astonishment. The righteous did not know whom they had served. The others did not know whom they had refused. Both groups are surprised. The difference between them is not knowledge. It is only what they did when they met need without a name tag on it.

The King's reply to them is the verified sentence the day's Angelus page also carries: "Just as you did not do it to one of the least of these, you did not do it to me". Omission is the charge. Not cruelty, not persecution, not dramatic wickedness — omission. The failure named here is the failure to act: the meal not shared, the visit not made, the door not opened. That is what makes the passage so uncomfortable for respectable people in every age, and what makes it so valuable. It relocates the gravest spiritual danger from the spectacular sins we congratulate ourselves on avoiding to the quiet non-events of an ordinary week — the person we meant to visit, the appeal we meant to answer, the stranger we meant to greet, tomorrow and tomorrow and tomorrow.

The scene closes with the two destinies stated in parallel, and then the Gospel moves on — immediately, in Matthew's next breath, to the plot to arrest Jesus and to the Passion. That placement is the final key to reading the passage well, and it tells us what kind of text this is. Matthew 25:31-46 is the conclusion of Jesus' fifth and last great discourse in this Gospel, the final teaching before the cross. It is not a timetable of the end of the world, offered to satisfy curiosity about dates. Matthew has already had Jesus refuse that curiosity outright. It is a parable-like judgment scene — a story told in the imagery of the last day in order to illuminate this day. Its tense is the future, but its address is the present. It tells its hearers: the meeting you are avoiding this week is the meeting the King counts as a meeting with himself. Read as a timetable, it produces speculation. Read as Jesus' last word before the Passion, it produces lunch invitations, prison visits, and opened doors.

That is also why attempts to use the passage as a weapon against other churches, religions, or people collapse under their own weight. Its surprises are aimed at everyone inside it; no group is complacent, and no group is excused. The only thing it rewards is mercy actually performed for people actually in need, and the only thing it condemns is mercy's absence. It is less a map of the next world than a mirror held up to this one. And the first two items in its list — with which any honest response must begin — are the most bodily needs of all.

## 4. I Was Hungry, I Was Thirsty: The First Two Works

The list begins with the stomach, and that beginning is itself a teaching. Before welcome, before clothing, before visits to the sick and imprisoned, there is hunger; and alongside hunger, its twin, thirst. Jesus does not begin with ideas. He does not begin with beliefs to affirm or rituals to perform. He begins with the two needs that every living body announces within hours of birth and goes on announcing, without embarrassment and without exception, every day of its life. A spirituality that cannot hear those announcements, the passage implies, has not yet begun.

To grasp the weight of hunger in the ancient world, it helps to set aside our modern assumption that food is generally available and its absence an anomaly. In the world of the Gospels, hunger was a standing condition of life for most people, not an emergency exception. Bread was not a side dish; it was the substance of survival, so much so that "bread" became the ordinary word for food itself, and the prayer Jesus taught asks for it daily — daily bread, because yesterday's bread does not feed anyone today. Harvests failed. Empires requisitioned grain. Widows, orphans, day labourers between hirings, and travellers far from home lived one misfortune away from an empty table. When Jesus says "I was hungry", his first hearers did not picture a metaphor. They pictured their neighbours, and some of them pictured themselves.

Thirst carried its own terror in a dry land. Water had to be drawn, carried, stored, and guarded. A well was wealth; a drought was a death sentence arriving slowly. To give someone drink was to give them a share of one's own life supply — which is why hospitality to the traveller stands so high in the moral world of both Testaments. The woman at the well in John's Gospel, Abraham running to meet three strangers in the heat of the day: again and again, the turning point is water offered or a meal prepared. Long before Matthew 25, Israel's Scriptures had been training the same instinct: the God who fed his people in the wilderness and gave them water from the rock expects his people to recognise hunger and thirst as holy occasions, not inconveniences.

Why should feeding come first among the works? Because feeding is the most basic form of recognising another person's life. Every other good we might do someone presupposes that they are alive to receive it. To feed a person is to say, in the most practical language there is: your life should continue. Your existence is not a problem to be managed but a life to be sustained. Philosophies can debate the dignity of the human person; a shared loaf simply enacts it. This is why the refusal to feed is so grave in the passage, and why Ezekiel's bad shepherds were condemned above all for letting the flock go hungry while they themselves ate well. The full table beside the empty one is the oldest picture of injustice the Bible knows.

The modern world has changed hunger's address without abolishing it. In wealthy cities, hunger often hides: the elderly neighbour rationing meals against a heating bill, the parent who eats less so the children eat enough. Elsewhere it does not hide at all. Famine still stalks regions broken by war and drought; the Pope's prayers at this Sunday's Angelus for drought-stricken Papua New Guinea belonged to the same Gospel sentence as the lunch that followed them. Between the famine zone and the city street stands the same simple fact: somebody, today, does not have enough to eat, and somebody else has more than enough. Matthew 25 does not permit its readers to treat that fact as weather. It treats it as a meeting with the King that is either kept or missed.

Against that fact, ordinary people have built an extraordinary architecture of mercy. The food bank in a church hall, sorting donations and filling boxes. The community kitchen where the meal is eaten at shared tables, not handed through a hatch. The school breakfast club that quietly ensures children begin the day fed. The relief agency airlifting grain into famine, and the neighbour who simply cooks double and takes a plate next door. These differ enormously in scale, and the passage makes no attempt to rank them. What it honours is the act itself: food moving from the hands that have it to the person who needs it. A cathedral of charity and a casserole carried across a landing are, in the King's accounting, the same kind of event.

Two cautions belong here, both from within the tradition itself. First, feeding must not become a way of keeping the hungry at arm's length: a donation can be genuine love, or occasionally a way of purchasing exemption from ever sitting at the same table. The lunch in the Paul VI Hall pointed the other way — the poor were not only fed, they were hosted. Second, those who serve must beware of making the meal about the server. The righteous did not know they had served the King; the healthiest feeding shares that spirit. The guest is honoured, the giver stays quiet, and nobody's dignity is the price of anybody's dinner.

There is, finally, a spiritual weight to eating together that leads toward the shared table at the centre of Christian worship. Human beings do not only eat to survive; they eat to belong. A table is where strangers become guests and guests become friends. It is no accident that Jesus' ministry was so often conducted at meals — eating with tax collectors and sinners, feeding multitudes on hillsides, breaking bread in an upper room on his last night. To feed the hungry is not merely to transfer calories. It is to extend the table: to declare that there is a place set for this person too. And thirst is never only physical in the Scriptures — the tradition that says "I was thirsty" also records a King who promised living water and who cried out in thirst from the cross itself. The one who judges the world's response to thirst has been thirsty. He knows the need from inside.

That knowledge — a King who has himself been hungry and thirsty — is the thread to hold as the reading continues. The works of mercy are not a ladder the strong climb to reach a distant God. They are the places where a God who came down is already waiting. The next works widen the circle further: to the stranger at the border of our community, and to the nakedness every human being longs to have covered with dignity rather than exposed to shame.

## 5. A Stranger and You Welcomed Me, Naked and You Clothed Me

The third and fourth works in the King's list move the scene outward. Hunger and thirst belong to the body at its most basic. The stranger and the naked person belong to the body in society — to the person who has no place, and to the person who has no covering.

"I was a stranger and you welcomed me," the King says, in the phrasing carried on the Vatican page for this Sunday. "I was naked and you gave me clothing."

Each clause is short. Each opens a world.

Consider first the stranger.

The Greek word behind the phrase is xenos. It can mean a foreigner, a guest, a traveller far from home, and also simply someone unknown. English splits those meanings apart. The ancient world held them together, because in the ancient world the unknown traveller was the test of a household and of a town.

Travel was dangerous. Roads were unsafe, inns were few and often disreputable, and a person away from his own kin had no protection in law or custom except the protection strangers chose to give him. To receive such a person — to bring him under a roof, to give him water for his feet, bread for the night, and a place to sleep where he would not be robbed — was not a decorative kindness. It was the difference between a journey survived and a journey that ended in violence or exposure.

Scripture remembers this duty again and again, and it remembers it from the inside. Israel is told, in paraphrase, not to oppress the stranger, because Israel knows the stranger's heart, having been a stranger in Egypt. The command is grounded in memory. A people that has itself lived without a home is forbidden to forget what that felt like once it has a home of its own.

Abraham, in Genesis, receives three travellers at the oaks of Mamre. He runs to meet them, bows, offers water, bread, and a calf prepared in haste. The narrative is told plainly, without commentary, and later tradition read it as a theophany — God received, unknowingly, in the form of guests. Whether or not every reader follows that later reading, the shape of the story is unmistakable: holiness arrives at the tent door disguised as need, and Abraham's greatness in the scene is not his theology but his speed. He does not deliberate. He runs.

The New Testament keeps the same thread. Hospitality — philoxenia, literally love of the stranger — is named among the ordinary marks of a Christian community. Elders are to be hospitable. Believers are urged, in paraphrase of the Letter to the Hebrews, not to neglect showing hospitality to strangers, for by doing so some have entertained angels without knowing it. The echo of Mamre is deliberate. The writer knows his readers know that story.

Notice what welcome is, in this tradition, and what it is not.

Welcome is not mere tolerance. Tolerance leaves the stranger outside, unharmed but unreceived. Welcome brings him in. It makes room — at a table, under a roof, in a schedule, in a budget. It costs the host something real: food, space, time, and a measure of safety, because to open a door is always, in some small way, to accept vulnerability.

And welcome, in the parable, is personal before it is structural. The King does not say, "I was a stranger and your nation had a policy." He says, "you welcomed me." That does not make structures irrelevant — laws and borders and systems shape millions of welcomes and refusals, and serious Christians argue, in good faith and sometimes sharply, about what just structures look like. The parable does not settle those arguments, and it should not be conscripted, by any side, as if it did. What the parable does is more searching than any policy: it asks whether, in the encounters actually placed before a given person, the stranger was received as a brother or turned away as a problem.

That question lands, today, in a world of displacement on a scale the ancient world could not have imagined. Wars, persecution, collapsing livelihoods, and disasters have driven vast numbers of people from their homes — within their own countries and across borders. Behind every statistic is the ancient figure from the road: a person far from kin, dependent on the protection strangers choose to give, sleeping where he is permitted to sleep.

To write about this reverently is to refuse two easy failures.

The first failure is to turn displaced people into symbols — illustrations for an argument, whether the argument is for openness or for restriction. A refugee family is not a debating point. It is a father who cannot protect, a mother calculating food, children learning early that the world can withdraw its floor. Any politics, of whatever colour, that uses such people merely as evidence has already failed the parable's test at the level of the heart, whatever its conclusions.

The second failure is to look away entirely — to treat displacement as weather, regrettable and unchangeable, best not thought about. The parable forbids that comfort. The King identifies himself with the stranger, not with the comfortable observer of strangers. Where the reader stands in the scene is therefore not a neutral question. In Matthew 25, there is no spectator's bench. There are those who welcomed, and those who did not.

Between those two failures lies the actual Christian task, which is unglamorous and local: the parish that sponsors a family and discovers it takes two years, not two weeks; the landlord who rents fairly when he could exploit; the teacher who learns a child's name correctly; the neighbour who does not let a new family eat its first festival alone. None of this resolves a global crisis. All of it is what "you welcomed me" looks like when it is translated out of the first century and into a street.

Then the King turns from the stranger to the naked.

"I was naked and you gave me clothing."

In the modern West, nakedness in this saying is often softened into a general image of poverty. In the ancient world it carried a sharper edge: shame.

Clothing was expensive. A cloak could be a man's most valuable possession, valuable enough that Israel's law forbade keeping a poor man's pledged cloak overnight, because it was his only covering to sleep in. To be naked, or nearly so, was not only to be cold. It was to be exposed — stripped of the dignity that clothing confers, marked publicly as destitute, unable to appear among others without humiliation. In a culture where honour and shame structured daily life far more than they do in most modern societies, nakedness was a social death before it was a physical one.

Scripture's long memory of clothing begins, strikingly, with God as the one who clothes. In the Eden narrative, after the fall, Adam and Eve sew fig leaves for themselves — inadequate coverings, made in shame and haste. The story then says, in paraphrase, that God made them garments of skin and clothed them. Even in judgment, even at the moment of exile, the Creator covers the shame of his creatures. The first tailor in the Bible is God, and his tailoring is an act of mercy toward people who have just disobeyed him.

That thread runs forward. Joseph's coat marks favour and provokes envy. The prodigal son, returning ragged and rehearsing his confession, is met — before the confession is even finished, in Luke's telling — with the father's order to bring the best robe and put it on him. The robe is not a reward for a good speech. It is the restoration of a son's standing, done in cloth. He will not stand in the courtyard in his rags while the household deliberates. He is covered first, and covered by the father's command.

Against that background, the scene at the cross is unbearable, and it is meant to be. The Gospels record, in paraphrase, that the soldiers divided Jesus' garments among them and cast lots for his tunic. The King of this Sunday's feast was himself stripped. He did not merely sympathise with the naked from a throne; he was numbered among them, exposed before a crowd, his last possessions gambled away at the foot of the instrument that killed him. When he says, "I was naked," he is not speaking in metaphor. He is remembering.

This is why clothing the naked, in the Christian practice that grew from this text, has never been only about warmth — though warmth matters, and a winter coat given in January is a serious thing. It is about restoring a person's ability to stand among others without shame.

Think of what clothing does, concretely. It lets a man attend a job interview. It lets a child walk into school without being marked. It lets a woman who has fled a violent home appear in a courtroom, a church, a workplace, as a person and not as a spectacle of ruin. The charities that understand this best do not hand out random bags of cast-offs at a door. They let people choose. Choice is part of the dignity: a person who selects her own coat, in her own size, in a colour she likes, is being treated as a customer and a neighbour, not as a disposal problem for other people's wardrobes.

So the stranger and the naked stand together in the King's list, and they belong together. Both are persons placed outside the ordinary protections — outside the household, outside honour. Both ask the same question of those inside: will you make room, and will you restore covering, for someone who cannot compel you to do either?

The sheep, as we shall see, do not remember doing it. That is part of the mystery still to come. But first the list darkens, from the road and the wardrobe to the sickroom and the cell — to the two works that cannot be done at a distance at all.


## 6. I Was Sick and You Visited Me, in Prison and You Came to Me

The last two works in the King's list are different in kind from the four that precede them.

Food can be sent. Drink can be delivered. A stranger can be welcomed by opening a door, clothing handed over in a parcel. But the King does not say, "I was sick and you sent medicine," or, "I was in prison and you posted bail." He says you visited me. You came to me. The verbs are verbs of presence. For these two works, there is no substitute for turning up.

That distinction is the whole teaching of this section, and it cuts against the grain of how help is usually organised now.

Modern kindness is increasingly remote, and much of it is genuinely good. Money moves across the world in seconds. A donation funds a clinic the donor will never see, staffed by people he will never meet, treating patients whose names he will never know. Nothing in the parable disparages that; feeding the hungry by funding those who feed them is still feeding the hungry. But the final two works guard a truth the others cannot carry on their own: there is a kind of human need that money cannot reach, because the need is not for a thing. It is for a person.

The sick know this.

Serious illness does something to a life that goes well beyond pain and diagnosis. It shrinks the world. The healthy move through wide spaces — work, travel, plans, quarrels, ambitions. The seriously ill move through a narrowing corridor: a bedroom, a ward, a waiting room, a scan, another waiting room. Friends' lives continue at full width, and the contrast is itself a form of suffering. News arrives from a world the patient can no longer enter. Invitations quietly stop, not from cruelty but from awkwardness — people do not know what to say, so, eventually, they say nothing, and the silence becomes its own diagnosis: you are now separate from us.

Loneliness of that kind is not a side effect of illness. For many patients it is close to the centre of it. Pain can be medicated. Fear can sometimes be answered with information. But the feeling of being quietly removed from the human circle while still alive — that is relieved by only one thing, and it is the thing the King names. Someone comes. Someone sits down. Someone's presence says, without needing to say it: you are still one of us; your world has narrowed, so I will enter the narrow place rather than wait for you to return to the wide one.

Visiting, understood this way, is presence rather than fixing — and this is where many would-be visitors fail, and know they fail, and stay away.

The visitor arrives determined to be useful and discovers there is nothing useful to do. He cannot interpret the scan. He cannot adjust the treatment. He cannot give the one piece of news the patient actually wants. Faced with that helplessness, the visitor often reaches for the tools he does have: cheerfulness, advice, stories of other people who recovered, brisk assurances that everything will be fine. The patient, who knows better than anyone that everything may not be fine, receives these offerings politely and feels more alone than before the visit. He has been managed, not accompanied.

The tradition of visiting the sick, at its best, knows a different art. It is largely the art of staying.

Staying means tolerating one's own uselessness in the room without fleeing into chatter or consolation. It means letting the patient set the terms — talk or silence, hope or honesty, prayer or ordinary news from outside. It means bringing the wide world in, in small doses the patient can hold: who won, who married, what the garden is doing, the joke from work — evidence that life continues and that the patient is still a citizen of it. And it means returning. A single heroic visit at the crisis is worth something. The fifth visit, in the third month, when the novelty has worn off and the ward is tedious and the conversation repeats itself, is worth far more, because it is then that abandonment actually threatens. Anyone can appear at the emergency. Love is measured at the follow-up.

There is a further reach to this work that should not be missed. Not all sickness is in hospital, and not all of it is in the body. The neighbour sinking into depression, whose curtains stay closed; the elderly man whose mind is going, whose conversation loops, whose old friends have stopped calling because calling has become sad; the mother at home with a chronically ill child, housebound for years inside a heroic routine nobody witnesses — these are the sick of an ordinary parish and an ordinary street. Visiting them rarely looks dramatic. It looks like a kettle, a chair, an hour. The parable dignifies exactly that scale. The King does not ask for cures. He asks whether anyone came.

Then the list ends in the hardest place of all.

"I was in prison and you visited me" — in the Vatican page's phrasing for the verse that completes the list, "I was in prison and you visited me."

Every previous work commands wide agreement. Feed the hungry — who objects? Clothe the naked, visit the sick — these are the common coin of every decent tradition. But the prisoner breaks the consensus, and he is meant to. He is the test case that separates mercy as a feeling from mercy as a practice.

For the prisoner may be guilty.

That sentence has to be faced squarely, because the parable's power depends on it. The hungry man might be lazy, the stranger might be dangerous, but the prisoner is the one figure in the list whom society has formally judged. A court has spoken. There may be victims — real people, with real wounds, whose claims on justice are sacred and must not be waved away by anyone's piety. The prisoner in his cell may be there because he did grave harm, and some of the harm cannot be undone in this life. Matthew 25 does not ask the reader to pretend otherwise. It does not say the prisoner is innocent. It does not say the sentence is unjust. It does not say the doors should be opened.

It says the King was there, and asks whether you came.

That is a different claim, and a more demanding one. It holds together two truths that our instincts prefer to separate: that justice matters, and that no human being — guilty, sentenced, confined — falls outside the reach of the King's identification with "the least of these." The prisoner remains a person. His punishment, however deserved, does not delete his humanity, and a society or a church that treats the confined as refuse has, by the standard of this text, refused a visit to Christ himself.

This is why prison visiting has stood, across the centuries, among the recognised works of mercy even when it was unpopular — and it has usually been unpopular. To visit a prisoner is to be suspected, faintly, of softness toward crime, of disloyalty to victims, of naivety about human nature. The visitor absorbs some of the prisoner's disgrace by association, the way the clother of the naked shares a little of the naked person's exposure. Perhaps that is precisely the point. Mercy that costs the giver no reputation is a cheaper thing than the mercy described here.

What does such visiting actually do? At the simplest level, it does for the prisoner what visiting does for the patient: it refuses the narrowing of a world. A cell is the narrowest corridor of all, and its deepest punishment is often not the regime but the message the regime delivers daily — you are forgotten; the world has agreed to proceed without you; your name survives, if anywhere, in a file. A visitor contradicts that message in person. For prisoners with no family, or whose family has finally, understandably, given up, a volunteer's face at the visits hall may be the only evidence that the world outside still contains a person who knows he exists.

Why, then, does this work frighten people most? The parable's list is shrewdly ordered, and its climax is not an accident.

Partly it is fear of the person. The sick man invites pity; the prisoner invites judgment, and judgment is a more comfortable emotion — it confirms the judge's own standing. To visit is to suspend, for an hour, the satisfying clarity of us and them, and to discover across a table someone disconcertingly like oneself: funnier than expected, more intelligent than expected, ruined by choices that began, often, in places uncomfortably close to one's own beginnings. Prison visitors commonly report that the experience unsettles their categories. That unsettling is not a defect of the work. It is the work.

And partly — this is the fear least often admitted — it is fear of what the visit reveals about the visitor's own life. Every person carries some knowledge of what he might have become under other circumstances: another childhood, another addiction, one worse night, one uncaught moment. The prisoner is a mirror most people would rather not look into, because he proves that the distance between the respectable and the ruined is sometimes much smaller than respectability requires. Visiting him means standing next to that proof for an hour, voluntarily. Many cannot. The goats in the parable, we will see, are not monsters. They are people who could not, or would not, go to certain places. The cell is simply the place where "would not" is easiest to defend.

It matters, finally, that the King lists prison last and does not make it optional. The works are a single garment. A faith that feeds and clothes and welcomes, but draws the line at the prison gate, has edited the list — and editing the King's list, in the world of this parable, is the one editorial decision with eternal consequences. The severity of that claim belongs to the next sections, and it will not be softened there. But it should be registered here, at the cell door, where it belongs: the least of these include the guilty. The King is found among people we have decided, with reasons that may even be good ones, to forget. And the question the text puts is not whether our reasons were good. It is whether we came.


## 7. When Did We See You? The Hidden King

Now the parable turns, and the turn is a question — the same question, asked from both sides of the judgment seat.

The righteous, in Matthew's telling, are bewildered. In paraphrase: Lord, when did we see you hungry and feed you, or thirsty and give you drink? When did we see you a stranger and welcome you, or naked and clothe you? When did we see you sick or in prison and visit you?

And the condemned, a few verses later, ask the mirror image of the same question. In paraphrase: Lord, when did we see you hungry or thirsty or a stranger or naked or sick or in prison, and did not minister to you?

Both groups are surprised. That shared surprise is one of the most important details in the entire passage, and it is easy to read past it.

The righteous did not know.

They were not performing for a hidden examiner. They did not feed the hungry while thinking, "This is Christ; this will count." They fed the hungry because the hungry were there and hunger is a reason. Their service was, in the strictest sense, unknowing — and the parable presents that unknowing not as a defect in their faith but as its very texture. They had so absorbed the habit of mercy that mercy had become unselfconscious, the way a healthy eye sees without thinking about seeing. Ask them to produce their record and they cannot. They were not keeping one.

This undoes a certain picture of religion completely.

In that picture, religious acts are a special category of behaviour — prayers, rituals, observances, donations announced and accounted — performed in sacred settings, consciously directed toward God, and recorded somewhere as merit. Ordinary acts belong to a different ledger: cooking, nursing, driving someone to hospital, keeping a spare room ready, sitting in a visits hall. The two ledgers run in parallel, and a person may be rich in one and poor in the other.

Matthew 25 collapses the two ledgers into one, and does so from the King's own mouth.

The acts by which human beings are finally judged, in this parable, are not the acts anyone would have filed under religion. They are meals. Cups of water. A door opened. A garment. A visit. Not one of them requires a sanctuary, a liturgy, a title, or even — this is the scandal the text does not hide — a correct theology in the mind of the person performing it. The sheep are not asked what they believed about the King. They are shown what they did about the least, and told that the King himself was the true recipient all along. "You did it to me" is the hinge of the whole passage: the service reached heaven by the earthly route, addressed to a hungry person and received by a King.

The reverse is equally exact, and it is carried in one of the short verified phrases the Church's own page preserves for this Sunday: "Just as you did not do it to one of the least of these, you did not do it to me" — verse 45. Neglect, too, travels by the earthly route. No one in the parable refuses Christ directly. No one slams a door in the King's face, mocks a crowned figure at the gate, or turns a recognisable Lord away from a sickbed. The failure is always at one remove: a hungry person unnoticed, a stranger unhoused, a prisoner unvisited — and the King, hidden inside that small figure, unmet. The tragedy of the goats is not blasphemy. It is inattention with consequences they never imagined, because they never imagined the King would be there.

This hiddenness is not a trick, and it is worth pausing to see why it is not.

A judge who disguises himself to catch his subjects out would be running an entrapment, and the parable would be a story about surveillance. But the King is not in disguise in the relevant sense. He is not pretending to be the least; he declares that he is identified with them — that what is done to them is, in the real structure of things, done to him. The identification is presented as a fact about the King's nature, not a costume he wears for the examination. He is the kind of king who counts an injury to the least of his subjects as an injury to himself, and a kindness to them as a kindness to himself. Earthly kings have sometimes claimed the first half of that in reverse — an injury to the king is treason against the realm. This King inverts it: an injury to the least is an injury to the crown.

Understood that way, the surprise of both groups stops being a puzzle and becomes the point. Of course the righteous did not see him. He was not visible as King in the places he had chosen to be found. He was visible as need. And of course the condemned did not see him either — they were looking, if they were looking at all, for someone whose importance would announce itself, someone whose service would obviously count. Importance, in the kingdom announced here, does not announce itself. It arrives hungry.

There is a deep consolation hidden in this for ordinary people, and it should not be passed over quickly.

Most human lives are not given dramatic vocations. They are given dependants, neighbours, colleagues, parents ageing, children hungry at ordinary hours, friends whose marriages or minds are failing quietly. The suspicion that haunts such lives — that real holiness happens elsewhere, among professionals of the spirit, in monasteries and missions and headline charities, while one's own kitchen and sickroom and spare bed are merely ordinary — is answered by this parable with unusual directness. The ordinary is where the King said he would be. The person who has spent twenty years feeding a household, sitting with the dying, receiving the difficult relative, visiting the embarrassing prisoner, and has never once thought of it as a spiritual achievement, is told at the end that none of it was beneath notice. It was the very thing. The least dramatic life in the parish may turn out, by this accounting, to have been conducted almost entirely in the presence of the King.

If the King is found in the least, then there is no encounter with the least that can be safely dismissed as insignificant. The calculus by which the world sorts people — this one matters, that one does not; this one's hunger is a crisis, that one's is background noise; this prisoner is a story, that one is a statistic — is precisely the calculus the parable abolishes. It does not abolish it by declaring everyone equally important in a vague, ceremonial sense. It abolishes it by relocating the throne. The King is not equally present everywhere in the parable's telling; he is specifically, deliberately present in the hungry, the thirsty, the stranger, the naked, the sick, and the imprisoned. Attention flows downhill in this kingdom, toward the lowest ground, and anyone who wants to find the King must be willing to go where the water goes.

Unknowing service and unknowing neglect, then, are the two great possibilities of an ordinary life, and both are happening constantly, mostly without drama. Every day presents its small least: the colleague eating alone, the new family on the street, the voice on the phone that needs twenty minutes instead of two, the hospital wing on the way home. Nobody can meet all of it; the parable is not a demand for omnipresence, and finite people with finite strength are not condemned by their finitude. But there is a difference — the whole difference, in this text — between a life oriented toward the least, which will miss much and grieve that it missed it, and a life oriented away, which misses the same things and never notices there was anything to miss. The sheep and the goats are not separated by their success rates. They are separated by their direction.

And still the question echoes. When did we see you? The answer the parable gives is the answer the whole of this article has been unfolding: you saw me at the table, at the door, at the sickbed, at the gate of the prison. You saw me every time. The only question the judgment finally asks is what you did with what you saw — or whether, seeing the least, you understood that you had seen enough.


## 8. Judgment That Is Salvation: The King Who Is No Tyrant

Everything in the parable now gathers at the judgment seat, and everything depends on what kind of king is sitting on it.

This was the burden of Pope Leo XIV's Angelus reflection for this feast, and it is worth stating in the brief's own verified terms: Christ is the measure of justice because he is the author of salvation. The Pope's point, paraphrased, is that this King is neither a despot who threatens nor a tyrant who crushes others with his power. His judgment is salvation. He stands alongside the least and the oppressed, and he measures justice by that standing — not by distance from them.

Hold that claim up against the scene in Matthew 25 and it illuminates the scene rather than softening it.

The judgment in this parable works in exactly the opposite direction. Nothing in it is theatre. No new evidence is produced, no hidden crime dramatically exposed. The King simply tells each group the truth about relationships that already existed — relationships they had formed, day by day, at tables and doors and sickbeds and prison gates, mostly without thinking about them at all. You did it to me. You did not do it to me. Judgment, here, is the truth of a life's relationships finally revealed as what they always were. The verdict does not create the reality. It discloses it.

That is why judgment can be called salvation without emptying the word of its seriousness. For the sheep, the disclosure is pure gift: a life of unregarded kindness is shown to have been, all along, a life lived toward the King. For the victims of the world's neglect — the hungry who were never fed, the prisoners who were never visited, the strangers who died unreceived — the disclosure is vindication. Someone has seen. The record was kept, not by a bureaucracy of merit, but by the King who was himself present in every one of those unnoticed encounters, receiving both the kindness and the refusal personally. A universe in which the least are finally told that their treatment mattered eternally is a more merciful universe than one in which their suffering is simply forgotten when they die. Judgment, in this sense, is what mercy looks like from the victim's side.

Now look closely at the charge sheet against the goats, because its contents are startling, and generations of readers have rightly lingered over them.

It contains no crimes.

No murder, no theft, no blasphemy, no adultery, no violence is listed. The entire indictment consists of absences: food not given, drink not offered, a stranger not welcomed, nakedness not clothed, sickness and imprisonment not visited. These are sins of omission, and the parable treats them — this is its most searching moral claim — as decisive. The goats are not villains. Nothing in the text suggests they were cruel, that they mocked the hungry or drove the stranger away with blows. They simply did not come. Their failure was a failure to act, repeated until it became the shape of a life.

And yet the text must be handled honestly at its hardest point, and a responsible reading will not flinch from naming it: the parable ends, in Matthew's telling, with a separation described in the language of eternity — the righteous into eternal life, the others into eternal punishment. Those words are in the passage. They have been read soberly by the Church's mainstream interpreters across the centuries neither as a threat to be wielded casually over others nor as a metaphor to be dissolved when it becomes uncomfortable. Readings differ — on how the imagery of the age to come is to be understood, on how this parable stands alongside the wider scriptural witness to God's mercy, on what may and may not be hoped for any particular person — and those differences are real, and belong to careful theology rather than to a Sunday article's paraphrase. What no honest reading can do is remove the passage's gravity. The parable insists that how the least are treated is not a minor topic, suitable for a charitable season and forgotten by spring. It carries, in the King's own framing, the weight of the last things.

How, then, should a reader stand before such a text — in fear, or in reverence? The distinction matters, and the Angelus reflection presses exactly here.

Fear, of the tyrant kind, cringes and calculates. It asks: what is the least I can do and still be safe? It turns the works of mercy into insurance premiums and the least of these into instruments of the giver's security. A religion driven by that fear will produce, at best, anxious compliance — and the parable's sheep are not anxious, and they are not compliant. They are surprised. You cannot be surprised by a verdict you spent your life calculating. Their freedom from calculation is the sign that something other than fear was driving them.

Reverence is different. Reverence stands before the King in awe of who he is — the one who identifies himself with the least, who was hungry and thirsty and stripped and confined, who will finally tell the truth about every life — and lets that awe become attention. The reverent reader does not ask how little mercy he can get away with. He asks where the King is to be found this week, and goes there, and finds, as the sheep found, that the going itself was never really a transaction. It was a relationship he had been in all along without knowing its name.

That, finally, is the shape of this feast, and the reason the Church sets this passage before her people on the Sunday of Christ the King. The crown in this Gospel is not an emblem of distance. The King reigns from the midst of the hungry. His court is the sickroom and the visits hall. His measure of justice is mercy enacted, and his judgment is the moment when the whole world learns what was true in every kitchen, doorway, and cell where the least were met — or missed — in his name without knowing it.

The parable leaves its readers with no spectators' bench and no neutral ground, but it does not leave them in despair. It leaves them with a list short enough to memorise and plain enough to begin before nightfall: someone hungry, someone thirsty, someone strange, someone shamed, someone sick, someone confined. The King who will judge by mercy has already told us where he waits to be found. He is not hiding to catch us out. He is standing, openly, with the least — and the door of that company, which is the only palace this King keeps, stands open to anyone willing to walk toward the lowest ground and begin.

## 9. A Feast Born in an Age of Tyrants: Pius XI and 1925

The Solemnity of Christ the King is, by the standards of the Church's calendar, a young feast. Most of the great celebrations of the liturgical year grew slowly out of the devotion of centuries, out of monasteries and popular piety and the slow accretion of custom. This one was instituted by a single act of a single pope, on a single date, in direct response to the political weather of a single decade. To understand what the feast means, and why its meaning has not faded in a hundred years, it helps to know the weather it was born into.

The year was 1925. Europe was seven years past the end of a war that had killed a generation and dissolved four empires. The old order of thrones and altars, which had seemed permanent within living memory, lay in pieces. In its place new powers were rising, and they were not modest ones. In Italy, Mussolini had consolidated a fascist regime that demanded the total allegiance of its citizens and treated the human person as material for the state. In Russia, a revolutionary government was building an officially atheist order that persecuted religion as a matter of policy. In Mexico, an anti-clerical government was moving toward the persecution that would soon ignite the Cristero conflict. Across the continent, nationalism was hardening into something worshipful: the nation, the race, the party, the leader were each, in their different ways, claiming the kind of devotion that earlier ages had reserved for God.

Pope Pius XI watched all of this with a theologian's eye for the underlying claim. The new tyrannies were not merely political arrangements he disliked. They were, in his reading, rival religions. Each of them announced, in effect, that some earthly power was ultimate: that history had a final meaning and that meaning was embodied in the state, the movement, or the man at its head. Against that claim, the ancient Christian confession that Jesus is Lord was not a piece of private piety. It was a direct contradiction. If Christ is King, then no earthly ruler is ultimate, and every earthly ruler stands under a judgment he does not control.

On 11 December 1925, Pius XI issued the encyclical letter Quas Primas, and with it instituted the feast of Christ the King. The document argued, in paraphrase, that peace would never be secure among peoples until individuals and states alike acknowledged the kingship of Christ; that the refusal to let Christ reign in public life, which the pope called laicism, was a plague eating away at the foundations of society; and that the remedy was not nostalgia for vanished thrones but a renewed public recognition that all authority is held in trust under a higher sovereignty. The kingship he proclaimed was not a programme for clerical government. It was a claim about where ultimate loyalty lies, and therefore about the limits of every lesser loyalty.

The encyclical was careful, too, about the character of the kingship it announced. Christ's rule, Pius argued, is primarily spiritual: a kingdom of truth, of holiness, of justice, love and peace, exercised not by coercion but by the attraction of minds and hearts won freely. Earthly kings rule by armies and laws and the fear of punishment. This King rules, in the encyclical's vision, by being known and loved. That distinction mattered enormously in 1925, and it matters now. The feast was never meant to baptise worldly power. It was meant to relativise it — to stand in the public square and say that the powers of this world, however loudly they demand worship, are not God.

Pius XI fixed the feast on the last Sunday of October, deliberately placing it just before the feast of All Saints, so that the kingship of Christ would be celebrated on the threshold of the Church's remembrance of the whole company of heaven. There it remained for more than four decades. In 1969, as part of the reform of the liturgical calendar under Pope Paul VI, the feast was moved to its present position: the last Sunday of the liturgical year, the Sunday immediately before Advent begins a new cycle. The move deepened the feast's meaning. Placed at the year's end, Christ the King became the Church's final word before the story starts again — the destination toward which the whole year of grace has been travelling. The readings assigned to the day in its new position took on an eschatological weight they had not carried in October: Ezekiel's shepherd, Paul's vision of the last enemy destroyed, and Matthew's great scene of judgment. The feast now closes the year by opening a window onto the end of all years.

A century later, the intention of 1925 has not expired, because the temptation it answered has not expired. The forms have changed. The rival ultimates of our own moment rarely march in uniform or demand salutes. They arrive as the total claim of the market, which prices everything and forgives nothing; as the algorithmic feed that wants not our taxes but our attention, hour by hour, and shapes our desires while it takes it; as the strongman politics that periodically returns, in country after country, offering frightened people a saviour who will crush their enemies; as the quieter idol of the self, sovereign and unaccountable, answerable to no judgment beyond its own preferences. None of these wears the face of 1925. All of them make the same claim Pius XI heard: I am ultimate; organise your life around me; there is nothing above me by which I might be judged.

It was this intention that Pope Leo XIV recalled at the Angelus in St Peter's Square on Sunday, 22 November 2026. Preaching on the feast in a year marked by continuing wars and by conflicts in which civilians bear the heaviest cost, he returned to the founding logic of the solemnity: that the Church proclaims a King precisely in a world of powers that crush, in order to say that crushing is not the last word about power. The King he presented, following Matthew 25, was neither despot nor tyrant but the one who stands alongside the least and the oppressed. The echo of 1925 was unmistakable and deliberate. Pius XI had instituted the feast so that the Church, surrounded by regimes that demanded worship, would annually confess where true sovereignty lies. A hundred years on, with different regimes and different idols but the same underlying contest, his successor stood in the same square and made the same confession in the same way: not by denouncing the powers at length, but by pointing to a King whose throne is a cross and whose court is the poor.

The year 1925 was also a Holy Year, a jubilee that drew pilgrims to Rome in great numbers, and the encyclical's timing was not accidental: Pius XI wanted the confession of Christ's kingship made not in a study but in a crowd, by ordinary believers on pilgrimage, at the very moment when the new regimes were staging their own mass rituals of loyalty. The twentieth century was discovering the political power of the festival, the rally, the choreographed crowd. The Church's answer was to use its own, older technology of public formation: a recurring feast day, observed in every parish, in which the faithful would sing, process, and pray their allegiance to a King whose emblem was a cross. It is worth noticing how completely the strategy refused the terms of its opponents. The regimes offered belonging through enmity — unity against the class enemy, the racial enemy, the foreigner. The feast offered belonging through worship of a King who commanded love of enemies. The regimes demanded that the individual disappear into the collective. The feast addressed the individual conscience and asked for a free act of allegiance that no state could compel and no state could supply. A century later, the technologies of mass loyalty have become subtler and more private — the rally has moved into the pocket and the feed — but the feast's counter-formation works the same way it did in 1925: one day a year, the community gathers and rehearses, in public, a loyalty that limits all others.

There is a lesson in the feast's history for how the Church argues with an age. Quas Primas did not respond to totalitarianism with a counter-totalitarianism, a Christian version of the same grasping after total control. It responded with a feast: a day of worship, repeated every year, in which the community rehearses a different account of what power is for. Arguments can be forgotten; a calendar is harder to forget. By embedding the confession in the rhythm of the year, Pius XI ensured that long after his encyclical ceased to be read, the Church would still, every November, be standing before the image of a crucified King and asking itself the feast's implicit question: whom do we actually serve? The question outlives every particular tyranny because it is addressed not to regimes but to hearts, and hearts in every age are tempted to hand their ultimate loyalty to something smaller than God.

## 10. My Kingdom Is Not of This World: Before Pilate and on the Cross

If the feast of Christ the King has a founding scene, it is not a coronation. It is an interrogation. The kingship of Jesus is defined, in the Gospels, at the moment of its most complete worldly failure: a prisoner, bound, standing before the representative of the greatest empire on earth, answering questions about what kind of king he could possibly claim to be.

The exchange in John 18 is worth following slowly, in paraphrase. Pilate, the Roman governor, asks the prisoner directly whether he is the king of the Jews. Jesus answers with a question of his own, probing whether Pilate asks from his own interest or merely repeats what others have told him. When Pilate presses the matter — what has he done, that his own people have handed him over — Jesus gives the answer that has echoed through every century since: his kingdom is not of this world. If it were, he explains, his followers would be fighting to prevent his arrest, as the followers of every earthly king fight. The fact that no army is coming, that no rescue is being mounted, that the prisoner stands alone and unresisting, is itself the evidence of the kingdom's nature. It does not come from this world's stock of power. It is not sustained by this world's methods. It will not be defended by this world's weapons.

Pilate, no fool, hears the claim underneath the denial and returns to it: so you are a king, then. And Jesus accepts the title on his own terms. He was born, he says, and came into the world for this purpose: to bear witness to the truth. Everyone who belongs to the truth listens to his voice. It is an astonishing redefinition. A king, in the world's grammar, is one whose word is law because he can enforce it. This king claims authority of an entirely different kind: the authority of truth, which does not compel but convinces, which does not arrest its hearers but awakens them. His sceptre is a voice. His subjects are not conscripts but listeners — those who, hearing the truth spoken, recognise it as the thing they were made for. Pilate's famous response, asking what truth is, hangs in the air unanswered, and the governor goes out to the crowd. The Gospel leaves the question standing, as if to say that the representative of worldly power can stand face to face with the truth and still walk away asking what it was.

What follows strips the title of King down to its bones and rebuilds it. The soldiers take the prisoner and stage a mock coronation: a crown woven of thorns pressed onto his head, a purple robe thrown over his shoulders, a reed for a sceptre, and blows and mockery for homage. Pilate presents him to the crowd in this costume of ridicule — behold the man, behold your king. And at the crucifixion itself, the title is nailed above his head in three languages, the inscription identifying him as Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews. Pilate, pressed to soften the wording to a mere claim, refuses: what he has written, he has written. The Gospel writers saw in this bitter irony a deeper truth being told against everyone's intention. The mockery was accurate. The cross was the throne. The thorn-crown was the real diadem of this kingdom, because this King's glory was never going to be the glory of domination. It was going to be the glory of a love that absorbs violence rather than returning it, that is lifted up not on a dais but on a gibbet, and draws the world to itself precisely from there.

Luke's Gospel gives the scene its most tender voice. In the tradition read on this feast, the crucified King is flanked by two criminals, and one of them, dying, does what no disciple in that hour manages to do: he recognises the kingship. Rebuking the other man's mockery, he acknowledges his own guilt, confesses that this man has done nothing wrong, and makes the simplest of petitions — that Jesus remember him when he comes into his kingdom. The answer he receives is among the most astonishing promises in all the Gospels, paraphrased here: that very day, he will be with the King in paradise. Consider the strangeness of it. The kingdom's first confessed citizen, after the apostles have fled, is a condemned thief. The King exercises his royal prerogative — the power of pardon, the ancient right of kings to show mercy — not from a throne of judgment but from a cross, not over a court but to a fellow sufferer, and the pardon is total and immediate. No probation, no penance first, no waiting to see whether the man deserves it. Grace arrives at the last possible moment, for the least likely of men, from a King who has nothing left to give except himself. He gives that.

This is the redefinition the feast exists to proclaim. In the world's kingdoms, power flows downward from the strong and is measured by how much can be commanded, extracted, and enforced. In this kingdom, power is redefined as self-giving: the King reigns by serving, conquers by forgiving, gathers his subjects by dying for them, and exercises judgment — as Matthew 25 will show — by identifying himself with the hungry and the imprisoned rather than with the tribunal. Before Pilate, the kingdom renounces the sword. On the cross, it reveals what it puts in the sword's place. Not weakness dressed up as virtue: the Gospels are clear that this renunciation is chosen, not imposed, the act of one who could summon more than twelve legions of angels and declines to. The meekness of this King is strength under perfect discipline, power that has looked at violence, understood it completely, and refused it — not because it cannot win that way, but because winning that way would lose the only victory it wants, which is the free allegiance of human hearts.

One more detail of the scene deserves attention: the inscription over the cross was written, John notes, in Hebrew, in Latin, and in Greek — the languages of religion, of empire, and of culture in that world. The Gospel writer lingers over the detail because it is a parable in itself. The kingship that the empire meant as a death notice was, without anyone intending it, proclaimed in every tongue that mattered, addressed to every sphere of human life: to the synagogue, to the state, and to the academy. And many read it, John adds, because the place of crucifixion was near the city. The first public announcement of this kingdom was not made in a temple or a palace but on a placard at an execution, read by passers-by. That is the pattern of this kingship throughout: it does its proclaiming in the places of shame, through instruments meant for mockery, and it entrusts its message to whoever happens to be walking past. Pilate thought he was labelling a corpse. He was publishing a reign.

That is why the title King, applied to Christ, can never be a simple compliment the Church pays its founder. It is a permanent criticism of every other use of power, including the Church's own. A community that worships a crucified King has a standard by which to recognise counterfeit authority wherever it appears: wherever power serves itself, punishes the weak, demands worship, and calls its cruelty strength, it has announced itself as belonging to the other kingdom, the one that is of this world. And wherever power spends itself for the helpless, tells the truth without an army behind it, and forgives at cost to itself, something of the true kingdom has broken in. The prisoner before Pilate is still, two thousand years later, the measure.

## 11. The Firstfruits and the Last Enemy: 1 Corinthians 15

The second reading of this feast, drawn from the fifteenth chapter of Paul's first letter to the Corinthians, supplies what the Gospel scenes presuppose: the ending. Matthew shows the King judging; John shows him reigning from a cross; Paul shows where the whole story is going, and in doing so transforms the emotional register of the feast. Without this reading, Christ the King could sound like a doctrine of threat — a sovereign returning to settle accounts. With it, the feast becomes what the Angelus of 22 November 2026 called it: a proclamation that judgment is bound up with salvation, because the Judge is the risen firstfruits of the very humanity he judges.

Paul's argument, paraphrased, begins from the resurrection as a harvest image. Christ has been raised from the dead, he writes, as the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep. In the agriculture of the ancient world, the firstfruits were the first sheaf of the harvest, offered to God not as a substitute for the rest but as its pledge and beginning: the same crop, the same field, the guarantee that the full ingathering would follow. Paul reaches for the image deliberately. The resurrection of Jesus is not, in his theology, a private marvel, a solitary exception to the human condition granted to one extraordinary man. It is the beginning of a general harvest. What has happened to him is what will happen, in their turn, to those who belong to him. The King who will judge the world is not an outsider descending on a humanity foreign to him. He is the first sheaf of humanity's own crop, risen ahead of the rest, carrying the rest with him as a harvest follows its firstfruits.

Paul sets this inside a great parallelism of two humanities. Death came into the world through a man, he argues, and through a man comes resurrection: in Adam all die, and in Christ all will be made alive, each in proper order. Christ first, then at his coming those who are his. The vision is corporate and patient. History is not a heap of disconnected individual fates but a story with a sequence, a harvest with a season, and the resurrection of Jesus is the turning point at which the direction of the whole story reversed. Before it, the human narrative ran one way, toward the grave, universally and without exception. After it, the narrative runs toward life — not yet visibly for everyone, not yet in full, but with the certainty that belongs to a harvest whose first sheaf is already in the barn.

Then comes the passage that gives the feast its cosmic scope. After the resurrection of those who belong to Christ, Paul writes, comes the end, when the risen King hands over the kingdom to God the Father, having destroyed every rule and authority and power opposed to God. He must reign, Paul says, until every enemy has been put under his feet — and the last enemy to be destroyed is death itself. The sequence matters. The kingship of Christ, in this vision, is not a static possession but a campaign with a terminus: a reign whose whole purpose is the progressive subjection of everything that enslaves, diminishes, and kills human beings, until the final and oldest enemy, death, is itself abolished. Only then, when the work is complete and the Son himself is subjected to the Father who subjected all things to him, comes the closing horizon of the chapter's vision: God all in all. The end of history, for Paul, is not a courtroom. It is a universe filled, without remainder or rival, by the life of God.

This changes the meaning of the judgment in Matthew 25 at its root. The King who separates the sheep from the goats is the same Christ who has died and risen as firstfruits, whose reign is defined by the destruction of death, whose endgame is God all in all. His judgment cannot therefore be read as the vindictive audit of a distant sovereign looking for failures. It is the judgment of the harvest's first sheaf upon the field he came to gather: a judgment exercised by the one whose entire mission is resurrection, whose standard of assessment — the works of mercy — are precisely the acts by which human beings participate, here and now, in his war against death. To feed the hungry is to push back death's frontier. To visit the prisoner is to deny that any human life is refuse. The judgment is hopeful not because it is lenient — the Gospel's words about those who refused mercy are solemn and unsoftened — but because of who is doing the judging and what he is doing in the world. The Judge is the enemy of death. The Judge has died and is alive. The Judge's final purpose is not condemnation but a creation in which God is all in all.

Paul's chapter also answers the fear that talk of resurrection is a consolation too thin for real grief. The Corinthians to whom he wrote were not theorists; they were a community burying its dead and asking whether the Christian promise survived the graveyard. Paul does not offer them a metaphor. He stakes everything, in the same chapter, on the resurrection being real, and he describes its manner with a farmer's patience: what is sown perishable is raised imperishable, sown in dishonour, raised in glory, sown a natural body, raised a spiritual body. The continuity and the transformation are both insisted on. It is this life, this body, these persons, that death does not get to keep. For a feast that ends the liturgical year, there could be no more fitting final word. The Church closes its year not with an epitaph but with a harvest festival: the first sheaf already gathered, the field still standing, the last enemy already named and already, in the risen King, sentenced.

There is one more movement in Paul's vision that the feast quietly depends on: the handing over of the kingdom. The risen Christ, having subdued every enemy, does not keep the kingdom as a possession. He delivers it to the Father. Kingship, at its eschatological climax, turns out to be stewardship — rule exercised for another, and surrendered when the work is done. No earthly ruler in the ancient world, and few in any world, could have imagined a sovereign whose final act of sovereignty is to hand the whole thing back. Paul could imagine it because the pattern was already set in the earthly life: the one who did not cling to equality with God, as another of Paul's letters puts it in paraphrase, emptied himself and took the form of a servant. The end matches the beginning. A King who reigns by self-giving finishes by giving the kingdom itself away, and the universe's last state is not a throne room with one occupant but a fullness in which God is all in all — sovereignty dissolved, if one may say it reverently, into communion. For the worshipper leaving church on this feast, the doctrine lands as a vocation as much as a prophecy: whatever small authority any of us holds — over a household, a workplace, a classroom, a community — is held the way this King holds his: in trust, for the life of others, and one day to be handed over.

Hold the three readings of the feast together and their harmony appears. Ezekiel promises a shepherd who will seek the lost and bind the injured. Paul promises that this shepherd's reign ends with death destroyed and God all in all. Matthew shows the shepherd-King, at the end, recognising his own flock by one unmistakable mark: they were the ones who, without knowing it, fed and clothed and visited him in his hidden presence among the least. The judgment is hopeful because the resurrection is first. The mercy is urgent because the harvest is real. And the King is credible as judge of all precisely because he has placed himself, body and biography, on the side of those who have the least — firstfruits of the dead, shepherd of the lost, and brother of the hungry.

## 12. The King in Every Being: A Vedanta Dialogue

A feast that proclaims a King hidden in the hungry, the stranger, the naked, the sick and the imprisoned will inevitably set a Vedantic reader thinking, because the recognition at the heart of Matthew 25 — the divine encountered unawares in the least of one's fellow beings — stands very close to one of the deepest currents in the Indian tradition. This closing section brings that encounter into the open, letting the two traditions meet honestly: naming where they genuinely converge, and where, just as genuinely, they part.

Begin with the Bhagavad Gita's teaching of equal vision. In the Gita's fifth and sixth chapters, the mark of the wise person is described, in paraphrase, as sameness of sight: the one who has realised the Self looks upon all beings with an equal eye, seeing the same Self in the learned and humble brahmin, in the cow, in the elephant, in the dog, and even in the one whom society counts an outcaste. The list is chosen to scandalise. It crosses every boundary its first hearers held sacred — learning, species, ritual purity — and declares that the deepest fact about every being in the list is identical: the same Atman dwells in each. The Gita is not offering a polite sentiment about tolerance. It is describing a perception that follows from knowledge: once the Self is known as the one reality within, the eye can no longer rank beings by their outer covering, because it has learned to see through the covering to the indweller. Elsewhere the Gita carries the vision further, presenting the perfected yogi as one who sees the Self abiding in all beings and all beings abiding in the Self, and who therefore cannot hate or grieve in the old way, having discovered that there is, at the deepest level, no other to hate.

The Isa Upanishad, among the oldest and most revered of the Upanishads, states the same vision in its most concentrated form. Its teaching, paraphrased, is that the one who sees all beings in the Self, and the Self in all beings, shrinks from nothing and no one thereafter; for such a seer, in whom all beings have become the very Self, what delusion and what sorrow can there be? The logic is exact. Fear and aversion depend on otherness: I fear what is not me, I despise what I judge beneath me. When the other is known as the Self wearing another form, the root of both is cut. The Upanishad does not command its reader to be kind as a moral exertion. It describes the kindness that becomes inevitable when the metaphysics is seen through. Compassion, in this frame, is not a virtue added to knowledge; it is knowledge, behaving.

The Ramakrishna tradition carries this vision from the text into the kitchen and the sickroom, and it is here that the dialogue with Matthew 25 becomes closest. Sri Ramakrishna, as his tradition teaches, was sharply critical of the phrase commonly used for charity, which spoke of showing pity to beings, and corrected it with a different doctrine: serve beings as Shiva. The point, as the tradition hands it down, is a revolution in the server's eye. Pity looks down; it confirms the distance between the fortunate giver and the unfortunate receiver. But if the being before you is Shiva — God himself in that form — then service is not condescension but worship, and the one served is not an object of charity but a temple. His disciple Swami Vivekananda took the teaching into organised action, making the service of God in man the signature work of the Ramakrishna movement: hospitals, schools, relief in famine and plague, all conducted under the conviction that the poor and the suffering are not merely to be helped but to be served as the divine itself, in the form tradition names daridra-narayana, God as the poor. The monk who might have spent his life in meditation found himself, by this doctrine, sent back into the slum and the ward — not as a distraction from God, but as the most direct approach to him.

Set this beside the Gospel and the convergence is real and should not be minimised. In Matthew 25, the King says that what was done to the least of his brothers and sisters was done to him. In the Ramakrishna tradition, the server is taught that the being before him is God in that form, to be worshipped by service. In both, the decisive encounter with the divine happens not in the sanctuary but at the point of human need. In both, the encounter is characteristically unrecognised in the moment: the righteous in the parable are astonished — they did not know it was the King they were feeding — and the Vedantic teaching likewise insists that the Self in beings is hidden, covered by name and form, seen only by the eye that knowledge has opened. In both, finally, the test of religion is relocated from profession to practice. The question at the judgment is not what the sheep believed but what they did; the question in the Gita is not what the devotee professes but how the devotee sees. Two traditions, arising worlds apart, agree that God is met in the neighbour or not met at all, and that a spirituality which passes the hungry by has failed at its centre, whatever its doctrines.

Honesty requires the differences to be stated just as plainly, for they are not small, and pretending otherwise would flatter both traditions by misunderstanding both. The first difference concerns identity. When the Christian King says that an act done to the least was done to him, he speaks as a person distinct from those he judges and from those he was served in: the hungry man is not, in Christian teaching, numerically identical with Christ; rather, Christ has freely bound himself to the hungry man in solidarity and love, so that the deed reaches him. The relation is one of gracious identification, not metaphysical identity. The Vedantic vision goes further along a different road: the Atman in the least is the Atman in the greatest, one and the same Self, and service is finally the Self serving itself through the play of forms. What Christianity expresses as the King's condescension — his free choice to be found among the least — Vedanta expresses as the seer's recognition: the discovery that there was never a second thing there at all.

The second difference concerns how the human predicament is healed. Christianity proclaims an incarnation: that God, at a particular point in history, became man, lived, suffered, died and rose, and that human beings are saved by grace — by a gift received from outside their own resources, through faith in that event and union with that person. The story is historical and unrepeatable, and its direction is God's descent toward humanity. Vedanta, in its classical Advaita form, proclaims a recognition: the Self was never bound, and liberation is the removal of the ignorance that mistook the Self for the body and mind — a dawn of knowledge rather than a rescue in history, God's presence discovered within rather than a gift arriving from without. Judgment and samsara mark the same contrast at the level of destiny. The Christian vision runs toward a judgment in history's consummation, a harvest, a kingdom handed over, God all in all — time fulfilled and ended in glory. The Vedantic vision runs toward liberation from samsara, the wheel of rebirth itself — time transcended, the soul's long wandering stilled in the knowledge of what it always was. These are not the same hope wearing two costumes. They are different maps of the human situation, and the dialogue is richer, not poorer, for saying so.

The dialogue also runs in the other direction at the level of practice, and both sides have something concrete to receive. The Vedantic discipline of equal vision gives the Christian reader a contemplative technology the parable assumes but does not teach: Matthew 25 tells the sheep that the King was there; it does not train the eye to see him there before the act. The Gita and the Upanishads are precisely such a training — a slow schooling of perception in which the server practises seeing the indwelling Self in the difficult, the ungrateful, the repulsive, until service stops depending on the worthiness or charm of the one served. Christian history has its own versions of this schooling, in the prayer that looks for Christ in the poor, but the Vedantic articulation of it, refined over millennia, is a genuine gift to receive. Conversely, the Christian vision offers the Vedantic server a sharpness about particularity: the King in the parable does not speak of humanity in general but of this hungry person, this prisoner, this stranger at this door, and the judgment turns on deeds done or refused in unrepeatable moments. Recognition of the Self in all can, in careless hands, drift into a serene generality that admires the divine in everyone and feeds no one. The parable forbids that drift. It nails the vision to a plate of food.

And yet the two maps, at the point where this feast stands, direct the traveller to the same doorstep. The Christian who has truly heard Matthew 25 and the Vedantin who has truly seen the Isa Upanishad's vision will meet — literally meet, in the same place: at the bedside of the sick person, at the prison gate, at the table where the hungry are fed, each unable any longer to treat the being before them as a stranger to God. One will say: my King is here, and I did not know it. The other will say: the Self is here, and I did not see it. The confessions differ; the astonishment is the same, and so is the act it produces. Perhaps that is the fitting note on which to end the liturgical year and this article: with two traditions standing before the same least brother or sister, each corrected by the other — Christianity reminding Vedanta that the divine who is found in all has also, in love, bound himself to each in particular, with a name and a history and a judgment of mercy; Vedanta reminding Christianity that the King who is served in the least is not absent from any being anywhere, that the vision of God in all is wider even than our creeds. The feast closes the year. The hungry are still there on Monday. Both traditions agree on what to do next.

## 13. Other Mirrors of the Least: A Comparative Reflection

A parable as stark as Matthew 25 does not belong to one tradition alone in its questions, even though its answer — a King who identifies himself with the hungry, the stranger, the naked, the sick and the imprisoned — is distinctively Christian. Other teachers, in other centuries and languages, have stood before the same human facts: that some eat and some go hungry in the same city, that power tends to serve itself, that a stranger at the door tests what a household really believes. This comparative reflection, drawn from the cross-tradition canon, sets eight such mirrors beside the King of Matthew 25. Each shows something true, and each shows by contrast what is peculiar in the claim that the judge of the world is found not above the least, but among them.

### Plato and the philosopher-king

Among world philosophers — the first domain of the canon — Plato offers the most direct contrast in kingship. In the Republic, his long conversation about justice, he argues that a city will have no rest from evils until those who truly know what is good are compelled to rule, or rulers are compelled to learn true philosophy. His philosopher-king is a reluctant ruler, educated for decades in mathematics and dialectic and in turning the soul from shadows toward reality itself. He rules because he understands justice, not because he craves honour or wealth. Justice in the city mirrors justice in the soul: each part doing its proper work, the whole ordered toward the good rather than private gain.

The convergence is real. Both Plato and the Gospel refuse the idea that kingship is licence. For Plato, the tyrant is the most miserable of men, enslaved to his appetites, while the just ruler is free because he serves an order higher than himself. For Matthew, the King is, in the Angelus's terms, neither a despot who threatens nor a tyrant who crushes others with his power. In either account a crown does not excuse cruelty. It increases responsibility.

The difference is just as instructive. Plato's king knows the good from above, by intellectual ascent, and orders the city downward from that knowledge. The lowest in his city receive their place in the order, but the philosopher does not identify himself with them; he governs them wisely without saying that what was done to them was done to him. Matthew 25 reverses the direction. The King is found not by climbing away from bodily need toward contemplation but by descending toward it with food, welcome, clothing and a visit. Plato corrects a lazy reading of the Gospel: mercy without a vision of justice, without an ordered city in which hunger is not endlessly reproduced, can become private sentiment. Matthew corrects Plato in return: an ordered city imagined from above can still miss the person in front of it, and knowledge of the good that never kneels beside a particular sufferer has not yet become justice in flesh and blood.

### Simone Weil and attention

Simone Weil, also in the domain of world philosophers, stands much closer to the parable's ground. A French philosopher of the twentieth century who worked in factories and died young, she placed one faculty at the centre of both prayer and justice: attention. For her, attention is not concentration. It is a waiting, an emptying of the self's noise, a willingness to look at what is real — above all at a person in affliction — without turning away, explaining it, or using it. Such attention, she argued, is exceedingly rare and closely akin to prayer, because in both a person sets aside self-importance long enough for reality to be received as it is.

Weil also gave affliction a precise meaning: suffering that crushes and uproots a life, makes a person feel discarded by God and by others, and renders its victim invisible — passed on the street, managed by institutions, discussed as a case, but not truly seen. That illuminates Matthew 25. The condemned are not accused of spectacular cruelty but of not doing: they did not feed, welcome, clothe or visit. Their failure begins earlier than the hand. It begins in the eye. They did not attend, and so they did not recognise.

Weil converges with the Gospel in insisting that love of neighbour is not first a feeling: a person may feel little and still attend, still ask what is needed, still stay. She also warns against consolation offered too quickly — telling an afflicted person that everything is meaningful, while offering no bread and no presence, can be a further affliction. Where Matthew completes Weil is in hope. Her writing can leave the afflicted soul in a darkness so complete that rescue feels almost impossible to describe. The Gospel, without softening the darkness, names a presence inside it: the King himself is there, in the least. Attention, in Christian terms, is attention to Christ hidden in pain, which changes despair into reverence without denying how heavy the pain remains.

### Mencius and the child at the well

From the domain of Chinese wisdom, Mencius brings a teaching about the beginnings of virtue inside every human heart. He taught that human nature carries four sprouts — tender beginnings that, if cultivated, grow into full virtues, and if neglected, wither. The sprout of compassion grows into benevolence; the sprout of shame and dislike into righteousness; the sprout of deference into propriety; the sprout of approval and disapproval into wisdom. None is the finished tree. A sprout can be starved by habit and neglect, as a seedling on a bare mountain can be cut down daily until the mountain looks as if nothing could ever grow there.

His most famous illustration is deliberately ordinary. A person who suddenly sees a small child about to fall into a well feels alarm and distress — not, Mencius argues, to win praise from the child's parents, not to gain a reputation, and not because crying is unpleasant to hear. The response rises before calculation. That spontaneity matters for Matthew 25. The righteous are surprised by the King's words; they produce no ledger of good deeds and seem hardly to have noticed doing anything remarkable. They fed and welcomed and visited, and only afterwards learn whom they served. Mencius helps explain that surprise: a cultivated sprout acts promptly, without first building a theory of its own goodness.

Mencius also insists that sprouts must be extended — from family to stranger, from impulse to settled care. A king who enjoys his parks while his people starve has failed to extend the feeling any decent person has at a well's edge. Matthew presses further: the parable is not only about a feeling to be cultivated but about a hidden identity to be discovered. The least are not merely occasions for compassion to grow; in them, the King says, he himself is met. Mencius gives the Gospel reader a psychology of mercy's first movement; the Gospel gives the Mencian reader a theology of the person toward whom it moves.

### Mozi and impartial care

Mozi, the other Chinese voice here, is more austere and more radical than Mencius in one respect. Writing in a China divided by warring states, he taught impartial care — regarding the welfare of others, other families and other states with the same serious concern one naturally gives one's own. He traced much disorder to partiality: people harm other households for their own, other states for their own, and call it loyalty. Against that he set a standard at once moral and practical: an action is judged by whether it benefits the world and increases what people genuinely need — food, order, security, life itself.

The convergence is striking at the point of the stranger. The parable's second work — "I was a stranger and you welcomed me" — breaks the boundary of household and kin, and so does impartial care, which refuses to make blood or birthplace the limit of obligation. Both distrust a love warm inside a small circle and cold outside it, and both judge professed belief by material outcome. Mozi would have little patience with devotion that leaves granaries closed. Matthew's King has none either.

The correction runs both ways. Mozi's standard, rigorously applied, can become a calculus in which persons are valued chiefly for benefit produced. Matthew 25 will not allow the least to be reduced to a calculation. The prisoner visited and the sick person tended may never repay or become useful in any ledger the world keeps. They are served because the King is in them, not because serving them optimises an outcome. At the same time, Mozi corrects a privatised reading of the Gospel: mercy cannot stop at individual kindness while public arrangements that manufacture hunger go unexamined. A bowl of soup matters. So does asking why the queue for soup grows longer every winter.

### Karl Marx, species-being and alienation

The domain of Marx and the critical tradition enters this reflection not as an enemy to be dismissed but as a critic to be answered honestly. Marx's early writing describes human beings through species-being: people are, by nature, makers and sharers, realising themselves in free, conscious, cooperative activity. Alienation names the distortion of that nature when workers are separated from the product of their labour, from labour itself, from other people, and from their own human possibilities. A person who produces plenty he cannot afford, in work that exhausts rather than expresses him, experiences the world he has made as a foreign power over him.

That critique lands close to the world Matthew 25 presupposes — a society in which some are hungry amid food, naked amid clothing, strangers without welcome. Marx asks the structural question the parable does not pause to ask: what arrangements keep producing the hungry, year after year, even when individual hearts are kind? His charge against religion follows. Where religion functions only as consolation — soothing the afflicted while leaving untouched the conditions that afflict them — it becomes, in his famous critique, a kind of anaesthetic. A faith that feeds no one, he would say, has mistaken its task.

Matthew 25 is the strongest internal answer Christianity possesses to that charge. The King does not commend those who consoled the hungry with words about a better world; he commends those who gave food. He does not bless those who explained the stranger's suffering; he blesses those who welcomed. In this text, faith that does not become bread, clothing and a visit is not faith that passes judgment — it is faith that fails it. Where Marx is right about religion that consoles without feeding, Matthew stands beside him, not opposite him.

Where the Christian account corrects Marx is at the level of what a human being finally is. In the critical tradition, the deepest wrong of hunger is that it alienates a person from his species-being, from the free and shared life that is properly human. In the Gospel, the deepest wrong is greater still: to refuse the least is to refuse the King himself. The hungry person is not only a fellow worker robbed of his humanity. He is a bearer of a hidden royal presence. That claim cannot be proved by economics, and does not replace economics, but it sets a floor no system may cross: a society may argue in good faith about how to organise production, yet on Matthew's terms it may not treat any person as refuse. Marx also corrects Christian readers in a way they should accept: a single act of feeding does not absolve anyone from asking whether the world that made the feeding necessary can be made more just. The parable judges persons. Marx interrogates systems. A mature reader needs both, in their proper order.


### Karuna and tonglen

The domain of Buddhist psychological techniques contributes a pair of practices that train the heart rather than merely instruct it. Karuna is compassion as a trembling response to suffering — not pity from a height, but the wish, grounded in felt nearness, that beings be free of pain and of its causes. It is cultivated deliberately, alongside loving-kindness, sympathetic joy and equanimity, so the response to suffering becomes stable rather than accidental: a person practices until compassion becomes a habit of perception.

Tonglen, the Tibetan practice of taking and giving, carries that training into difficult territory. The practitioner breathes in, imaginatively taking in another's suffering — fear, sickness, loneliness, shame — and breathes out relief, space, warmth and whatever would genuinely help. The practice reverses self-protection: ordinarily we breathe in what is pleasant and push away what hurts; tonglen trains the opposite movement, in small, bearable doses, until the heart learns it need not close at the first sight of pain. Its teachers ground it in ordinary kindness: the imagination is rehearsed so the hands will follow.

The convergence lies in that reversal. The righteous have moved toward suffering rather than away from it — toward prisons, sickrooms and roadsides. Tonglen gives a Christian reader a disciplined account of how such movement becomes possible without pretending it is easy. Compassion, it also shows, is weakened less by malice than by avoidance.

The tension is genuine. Tonglen's final horizon is the liberation of all beings from suffering and the cycle that produces it; Matthew speaks of a personal King who identifies himself with the sufferer and will judge. The Christian does not merely exchange breath with the least; he meets someone in them. Conversely, judgment can make compassion sound like a transaction for a future verdict, and Buddhist practice corrects that distortion by insisting compassion is worth cultivating for its own sake, with no audience and no reward in view. Held together, the two purify each other: tonglen teaches the Christian how to practice staying; Matthew teaches the practitioner whom he is staying with.

### Naikan and the three questions

From the domain of Japanese psychological arts comes Naikan, a structured practice of self-reflection developed in twentieth-century Japan from older currents of Japanese Buddhist self-examination. Its form is simple. A practitioner recalls a specific person — a mother, a teacher, a spouse, a colleague — over a specific period, and examines that relationship through three questions, in order. What did I receive from this person? What did I give to this person? What trouble did I cause this person? The questions are asked concretely — actual meals, journeys, money, time, patience and care — not as general impressions.

The power of the practice lies in the order of the questions. Most people, recalling a relationship, begin with what they gave and what trouble the other person caused them. Naikan reverses the ledger. Beginning with what was received — usually a far longer list than expected — reorders memory itself: a person who believed he had largely raised himself discovers the countless hands that fed, clothed, taught, forgave and carried him. The third question is the sharpest: not what trouble I suffered, but what trouble I caused. Asked without self-hatred or evasion, it produces not shame as a weapon but sober gratitude and a wish to repair.

Set beside Matthew 25, Naikan illuminates the parable from the side of the examined life. The King asks what we did for the least; Naikan asks what was done for us, and what we failed to notice while it was being done. A person formed by the three questions will not imagine mercy as a favour descending from the self-sufficient to the needy: he knows he has been the needy one, repeatedly, and that much of what he received arrived unearned and unthanked. That removes condescension from mercy. The one who feeds has himself been fed; the one who visits knows what it is to wait for a visitor who does not come.

Naikan's limit is its horizon. It can heal memory and awaken gratitude, yet it does not by itself name the stranger outside remembered relationships; its questions are retrospective, while the parable's demand is also prospective, toward people one has never met. Matthew extends Naikan's honesty outward, and Naikan extends Matthew's demand inward: before asking how much mercy I have shown, ask how much I have received and failed to register.

### Zengzi and daily self-examination

The final mirror comes from the domain of Chinese psychological techniques, in the Confucian practice of self-cultivation recorded in the Analects through Zengzi, a disciple of Confucius. Zengzi describes examining himself daily on three points. In dealings on behalf of others, has he been fully loyal and trustworthy? With friends, has he been sincere and true to his word? And has he failed to practice what he was taught? The examination is brief, regular and unsparing. It assumes character is not a possession but a cultivation — tended daily, like a field, or allowed to deteriorate daily, by the same small increments.

Self-cultivation in the Confucian tradition is wider than this one practice — it includes study, ritual propriety and the slow alignment of inner feeling with outward conduct, from the family outward to the state. But Zengzi's three points distill its method: short accounts, kept daily, before failures harden into character. Each question has a definite object — service rendered for another, words spoken to a friend, teaching received and either practiced or shelved.

The resonance with Matthew 25 is methodological as much as moral. Behind the King's commendation stands a lifetime of small fidelities, most unnoticed even by those who performed them. Zengzi shows how such a lifetime is built: by examination frequent enough to catch drift early. His third question is closest to the parable's nerve. To have heard a teaching — about mercy, say — and not practiced it is a failure to be named that same day, not deferred to a final reckoning in hope the reckoning will be lenient. Read alongside Zengzi, Matthew's judgment stops looking like an ambush. It looks like the last of many examinations, one a person has either been rehearsing honestly for years or avoiding for years.

The correction runs both ways. Self-cultivation can become a project of self-perfection in which others figure mainly as occasions for one's own refinement. Matthew 25 forbids that use of the neighbour: the least are not instruments for anyone's virtue, but the place where the King is met. Zengzi, in turn, corrects a passive reading of the Gospel that waits for a great moment of mercy while neglecting the daily audit that makes mercy ready. The King is met on ordinary days, by people whose examination the night before left them a little more honest.


## 14. Honest Difficulties: Fear, Merit and the Limits of One Pair of Hands

A teaching this demanding deserves honest difficulties. Matthew 25 has been used to frighten the tender-hearted, to burden the already burdened, and to turn the poor into instruments in other people's spiritual dramas. It has also been quietly set aside by readers who found its standard impossible and decided it could not mean what it appears to mean. Both responses fail the text. This section takes up the difficulties in turn — fear, merit, scale, exhaustion and exploitation — and tries to say what the parable does and does not demand.

The first difficulty is motive. If the King separates sheep from goats on the basis of these works, is mercy performed from fear of punishment still mercy? A person who feeds the hungry chiefly because he dreads the goats' sentence acts partly from self-interest, and self-interest seems to spoil the gift. But human motives are almost never single. A parent who runs into a road for a child acts from love, habit, instinct and terror at once, and no one audits the proportions before calling the act good. Moral traditions recognise stages in motive's growth: a person may begin by avoiding punishment, continue by valuing the good he has tasted, and end — if grace and practice do their slow work — by loving the neighbour for the neighbour's own sake. Fear is a beginning and a guardrail, not a destination; it corrupts only when terror is preferred because terror is easier to administer than love.

The parable itself points beyond fear. The righteous are surprised, and that detail does much theological work. People acting from calculated fear are not surprised to be rewarded; they have kept accounts precisely in order not to be. The righteous appear to have forgotten their own deeds, which suggests the deeds flowed from character rather than calculation — Mencius's cultivated sprout, Zengzi's examined life, attention practised until it becomes reflex. The passage therefore describes love-driven service and gives fearful readers a place to start. Starting from fear and ending in love is not hypocrisy. It is education.

The second difficulty is the old tension between faith and works, to be handled even-handedly, since sincere Christians have stood on both sides with Scripture in hand. Read in isolation, Matthew 25 can sound as though salvation were a wage — so many meals and visits tallied and exchanged for a kingdom. Read in isolation the other way, parts of the New Testament can sound as though conduct were irrelevant beside correct belief. Neither isolated reading survives the whole witness. The parable does not present the works as purchases: the kingdom, in the King's own framing, is an inheritance prepared long before the deeds — and an inheritance is received, not bought. The works reveal not a price paid but a life recognised; they are the visible shape of a love that was real. Conversely, the insistence that salvation is gift has never, in the tradition's mainstream, licensed indifference to the neighbour. A gift that never becomes bread in someone else's hands is a gift whose reception is itself in question. Faith and works describe one thing from two sides: trust in the King, and the form that trust takes when it meets a hungry person. The parable is severe with the second side because its audience was tempted to neglect exactly that side. Severity toward a neglected duty is grace refusing to be used as an alibi.

The third difficulty is scale. Hunger today is structural as well as personal — woven into supply chains, land arrangements, wars, debts, climates and markets no individual chose and none can repair alone. One pair of hands can feed a street, not a famine. Does the parable then condemn every comfortable reader for every distant hunger he did not relieve, in a guilt without limit and therefore without meaning?

Precision matters. The parable speaks in the grammar of personal encounter: the one in front of you, hungry, strange, unclothed, sick, imprisoned. Its direct demand falls on what is within reach — the person actually met, the need actually known, the resources actually held. It is not a theory of global economics, and inflating it into one produces either despair or dismissal. But neither does it seal mercy inside private charity. A person who feeds the one in front of him, honestly and repeatedly, will begin to ask why so many stand in front of him — and that question leads to the shared and civic forms of mercy: supporting what reliably feeds people and refusing what reliably starves them. Mozi and Marx press exactly here. The parable does something prior to their work: it forms people who cannot walk past. What such people then build together is a matter of prudence and vocation. No one is commanded to solve hunger alone. Everyone in the parable's hearing is commanded not to be innocent by avoidance.

The fourth difficulty is compassion fatigue, modern in wording but ancient in experience. Nurses, carers, aid workers, parents of chronically ill children and ordinary people who live near great need know the state in which feeling itself wears out: the sufferer still suffers, but the helper's heart responds more slowly, then with irritation, then with a numbness that feels like betrayal. If Matthew 25 is read as a demand for unlimited emotional output, fatigue becomes guilt, and guilt becomes either frantic overwork or collapse.

The parable, read carefully, demands deeds more than feelings. It does not ask whether the righteous felt compassion at a specified intensity; it records that they gave food, welcome, clothing and presence. Feelings ebb in every long obedience; hands can continue while the heart recovers, and the heart often recovers through modest, sustainable action rather than heroic exertion followed by ruin. Rest belongs in the pattern too — the God of the wider Scripture rests and commands rest, and a servant who refuses all rest is not imitating that God but attempting to replace him. The person who has given what he genuinely has, and stopped at the edge of it, has not failed the parable. He has obeyed it within creaturely limits.

The fifth difficulty is the subtlest and most dangerous: using the poor as instruments of one's own salvation. If serving the least earns the King's commendation, a calculating heart can treat hungry people as spiritual currency — opportunities for merit, material for a testimony, props in the drama of one's own redemption. The poor have long experience of this: photographed in their need, helped in ways that advertise the helper, dropped when the helper's project moves on. The parable's own logic destroys the practice it is sometimes used to justify.

The destruction is in the identification itself. The King does not say the least are a means by which he may be reached, as a toll is a means to a road. He says what was done to them was done to him. The hungry person is therefore not an instrument in the encounter; he is, in the King's claim, its principal party. To use him as a prop is to miss the very presence the parable announces. Practically, this means the helped person's dignity, privacy and preferences govern the manner of helping; no story or image of a sufferer is shared for the helper's glory; and help continues, where possible, when no one is watching and nothing is gained — exactly the condition of the righteous, who did not know. Their surprise is the parable's built-in protection against exploitation: those who serve best are those who have forgotten to keep score.

None of these difficulties dissolves the demand. Fear is a beginning, not a home. Works are evidence, not currency. Scale enlarges responsibility without making it infinite. Fatigue is a limit to be honoured, not a sin. And the poor are persons, never props. What remains is the original claim: the King is met among the least, and the meeting is the judgment. Honesty about the problems does not make that claim easier. It makes it cleaner — and a clean demand, unlike a frightening or flattering one, can actually be obeyed.


## 15. A Seven-Day Practice of the Least

Teaching that never becomes practice evaporates, however beautiful it sounds on a Sunday. What follows is a seven-day practice built on the works named in Matthew 25 — feeding, welcoming, clothing, tending the sick, visiting the confined — with a day of examination drawn from Naikan and Zengzi, and a closing day at a shared table. Each practice is deliberately modest. The measure of the week is not heroism; it is whether each thing was actually done, by an ordinary person, inside an ordinary schedule. A small work completed teaches more than a grand work indefinitely intended.

**The first day: feed.** Choose one concrete act of feeding and complete it before the day ends: a meal prepared for someone whose week is harder than yours, extra bought at the market for a food bank, a contribution to a kitchen whose work you have actually looked into, or company for a person who usually eats alone. Keep it specific — a named person or place, a real time, real food. If money is the form it takes, give to work you can describe, not to an abstraction. And notice how unremarkable it feels. That is the point. The righteous did not feel they were performing wonders. They were doing what was in front of them.

**The second day: welcome the stranger.** The stranger is whoever stands at the edge of your usual circle today: the new colleague whose name no one has learned, the family newly arrived on the street, the visitor who sits at the back and leaves quickly. The practice is welcome proportionate to your real position — an introduction made properly, a name learned and remembered, a door genuinely opened rather than politely gestured toward. Welcome differs from tolerance. Tolerance leaves the stranger where he is and congratulates itself for not objecting. Welcome moves, speaks, makes room. One real conversation fulfils the day.

**The third day: clothe.** Identify clothing that is good — genuinely good, not worn out — and more than you need, and pass it where it will actually be worn: a shelter, a charity that outfits people for work, a family you know is stretched. The discipline is in the quality; giving what is fit only for disposal is housekeeping dressed as mercy. Notice, too, whether anyone in your actual orbit is underdressed against the weather, and remedy it if it is yours to remedy. Clothing in the parable is about dignity as much as warmth: a person decently clothed can walk into a room as an equal.

**The fourth day: tend the sick.** Visit, call or sit with someone who is ill, recovering, or living with a condition that has narrowed their world. The practice values presence over usefulness. Much of what the sick endure is not pain alone but disappearance — the slow discovery that life goes on elsewhere without them. A visit interrupts that discovery. If visiting in person is impossible, an unhurried phone conversation is the day's work; a brief message sent to discharge an obligation is not. Ask what is needed and listen for the actual answer, which may be practical — a lift, a prescription collected — or simply news of the outside world and an hour of company. Do not offer medical opinions or explanations of the illness's meaning. Bring presence, and let it be enough.

**The fifth day: visit the confined.** Imprisonment in the parable names literal prisoners, and where a reader has a genuine route to prison visiting or to supporting prisoners' families — who serve a sentence of their own outside the walls — it should be taken seriously and followed properly. For many, the day's honest form will be wider: the housebound neighbour who has not left home in weeks, the person in a care facility whose visitors have dwindled, the acquaintance confined by depression or disgrace whom everyone else has quietly stopped contacting. Confinement is defined by the walls a person cannot cross alone. Choose one such person, cross toward them, and stay a while. The visit that costs a little awkwardness is usually the one this day is about.

**The sixth day: examine and rest.** Give Naikan and Zengzi half an hour of quiet. First, Naikan's three questions, directed at the week just lived and at one or two people who carried you through it: What did I receive? Name the meals, the patience, the invisible work, the forgiveness. What did I give? Answer briefly, without inflation. What trouble did I cause? Answer honestly, without drama — the sharp word, the forgotten promise, the need walked past. Then Zengzi's points in spirit: In what I undertook for others, was I wholehearted? In my words, was I true? What I have heard and read — this article included — have I practiced, or only admired? Write the answers down if writing helps; the page keeps us honest in ways memory avoids. Then stop. The day is also for rest: a meal taken slowly, work set down, the examination closed rather than brooded over. Examination that ends in rest produces gratitude and resolve; without rest it produces only a more refined anxiety.

**The seventh day: the shared table.** End the week by eating with others, and let the table be wider than habit makes it. Invite someone from the week's encounters, if invitation is natural and welcome, or someone long at the edge of your circle. Cook simply and generously; the feast is in the company, not the menu. If a table at home is not possible, join one that exists — a community meal, a congregational lunch — and take a seat among people you did not choose. At the table, practice the week's lessons lightly: attend, as Weil taught; receive gratefully, as Naikan taught; notice who is missing, as the parable teaches. No speech is required. The week that began with feeding one person ends with the discovery that feeding and being fed are, at a shared table, almost the same act — perhaps the nearest a practice can come, in seven days, to the kingdom the King describes.


## 16. The Table at the End of the Year

This Sunday stands at a threshold in the Church's year. The Solemnity of Christ the King closes the liturgical year: the long journey through the life, teaching, death and resurrection of Jesus arrives at its final scene — and the final scene is not a coronation portrait but a judgment. Yet that judgment, as Matthew 25 shows, has the character of a feast as much as of a tribunal. The King who judges has identified himself with the least, and the verdict over the righteous is an invitation: enter, inherit, come to the table prepared. A year ending with such a judgment ends, for all its severity, in welcome. Its last word is a door held open toward a kingdom.

Next Sunday, Advent begins, and the Church turns from the King's return to the King's coming — from the end of the story to its beginning, from judgment to waiting. The turn is no retreat from this feast. The one awaited in Advent is not an unknown power whose character must be guessed at; he is the child whose grown life ends, in Matthew's telling, on a throne of judgment that turns out to be a place among the hungry and the imprisoned. To wait for him well is therefore not passive. It is this article's seven days' practice extended across a season — feeding, welcoming, clothing, tending, visiting, examining — the works by which the waiting Church recognises the one it waits for, having learned where he may be found.

An image from today itself gathers the whole passage into a single scene. At the Angelus in St Peter's Square this morning, Pope Leo XIV reflected on this same Gospel — on Christ as the measure of justice because he is the author of salvation, on a King who is neither despot nor tyrant but stands alongside the least and the oppressed. After the Angelus, he joined a lunch with some 1,300 poor people in the Paul VI Hall, held for the World Day of the Poor. The reflection named the teaching; the lunch enacted it; and the distance between the square where the words were spoken and the hall where the meal was shared turned out to be a short walk. That is Matthew 25's whole argument in one morning's itinerary: the King is proclaimed, and then the King is met — at a table, among the least, in the most ordinary act by which human beings recognise one another: eating together.

It seems fitting that a year of readings should close there, and a new year of waiting begin there too — not in a throne room, but in a hall full of tables. The feast of Christ the King does not ask its hearers to admire a distant majesty. It asks them to learn an address, given in the plainest terms: among the hungry and the stranger, the unclothed and the sick and the confined, in the least of his brothers and sisters. Whoever goes to that address will find that the judgment they feared has the strangest quality — it looks, from close at hand, remarkably like an invitation to lunch. And the year turns, and Advent's first candle waits to be lit, and the table is already being laid.
