There is a particular kind of silence that gathers around an object small enough to hold in two hands and old enough to outlast empires. This week that silence has a name and an address: the Golden Pagoda at Namsai in Arunachal Pradesh, where the corporeal relics of Shakyamuni Buddha, preserved for decades in the stillness of a museum in Delhi, have been carried across the mountains of the northeast and set down among people for whom the Buddha is not a figure in a book but a living presence to be welcomed home.

The paradox is one the Buddha himself prepared us for. No teacher in history was clearer that everything compounded falls apart, that the body is not the self, that clinging even to the most sacred form is a cause of sorrow. And yet no teacher's remains have been guarded, divided, enshrined, carried over deserts and seas, and wept over with gratitude by more generations than his. Between those two truths — the doctrine of impermanence and the devotion of the reliquary — lies one of the most instructive tensions in all of Buddhism, and one of the most human stories in the whole life of religion.

This article follows that tension wherever it leads. It begins with the news from Arunachal Pradesh, then walks back to the night at Kushinagar when the Buddha breathed his last and his followers had to decide what, if anything, should remain. It visits the stupas of Ashoka and the excavations at Piprahwa, asks honestly what a relic is and what it is not, and lets the reliquary preach its own quiet sermon on impermanence. It also listens to the neighbours: to Vedanta, which honours presence in consecrated form and counts the Buddha himself among the avatars, and to the other great traditions that keep the remains of their holy ones. It ends, as every act of veneration should end, not at the shrine but in practice — seven days of it, offered plainly.

Before the history, though, sit for a few minutes with a teacher of another generation who spent his life serving holy objects and the beings who venerate them. In this teaching extract, Lama Zopa Rinpoche explains why, in the Buddhist understanding, every holy object is received from the kindness of every sentient being — a fitting doorway into a day spent with what the Buddha left behind.

## 1. A Sacred Arrival at the Golden Pagoda

In the far north-east of India, where the plains of Assam rise toward forested hills and the borders of another world of mountains, a quiet procession has been moving through Arunachal Pradesh this autumn. The Press Information Bureau, Government of India (https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2330300&reg=6&lang=1), reporting for the Ministry of Culture, describes an exposition of the Holy Relics of Shakyamuni Buddha in the state from 31 October to 21 November 2026. It is not a museum loan in the ordinary sense, and it is not received as one. In the towns through which the relics pass, they are welcomed with prayer, with offerings of flowers and light, and with the kind of hushed attention that belongs to the arrival of a revered teacher rather than to the transport of an ancient object.

The itinerary given in that report is precise. The relics were first taken to Tawang, high in the western Himalaya, where they remained from 1 to 7 November. Tawang is inseparable in the public mind from its great monastery, founded in the seventeenth century and long a centre of Mahayana learning for the Monpa people, and a place where the presence of the Buddha is already woven into daily work, study and prayer. From Tawang the exposition moved to Bomdila, from 8 to 13 November, another hill town with its own monastery and its own community of devotees for whom a journey to a distant shrine may be the labour of a lifetime. Then, on 14 November 2026, the relics are transported to Namsai and installed at the Golden Pagoda, with public darshan from 14 to 20 November.

Namsai gives the arrival a different colour. The Golden Pagoda, known as Kongmu Kham in the Tai Khamti language, rises in the Burmese-inspired style cherished by the Theravada communities of the district. Its gilded tiers catch the low sun over the plains, and around it live Khamti, Singpho and other peoples whose Buddhism came not over the Himalaya from Tibet but up the river valleys from Myanmar and Thailand. That one exposition can be received with equal devotion in Tawang, in Bomdila and in Namsai is itself a small lesson in Buddhist history. The tradition has never had a single face. It has learned many languages, built in many styles, and carried the memory of its teacher along trade routes, pilgrim paths and royal roads into almost every landscape in Asia. Yet the focus remains the same figure, and the same fragments of a human body cremated more than two thousand years ago near a town called Kushinagar.

Darshan is a word that deserves a pause. It means seeing, and being seen. A devotee does not merely inspect a reliquary through glass. He or she stands before it, folds the hands, bows the head, and for a moment allows the whole scattered business of life to gather into a single act of attention. The old, the very young, monks in maroon and saffron, laypeople who have driven for hours, officials who must for a while lay aside their files — all enter the same queue. For many, this will be the closest they ever come to the physical trace of the Buddha. For all, if the tradition is allowed to speak in its own terms, the point is not possession. No one takes the relic home. One takes home only what the encounter has stirred: a memory sharpened, a resolution renewed, a grief softened, a practice resumed.

That is why this section must stay brief, even though the event matters. The news is a doorway, not the house. A government report can tell us dates, places and arrangements. It cannot tell us why a fragment of bone should move a grown person to tears, or why a community should prepare for weeks to receive something so small that it could rest in the palm of a hand. To understand that, we have to go back — back to a grove of sal trees in the fifth century before the common era, to a teacher lying down between twin trees to die, and to the strange, tender instructions he left about what should be done with his body when he was gone.

For the paradox is there from the beginning. No teacher in the ancient world insisted more clearly on impermanence. All conditioned things, the Buddha taught throughout his life, arise and pass away. Bodies age, sicken and die. Empires crumble. Even teachings, carried in fallible memory and copied by fallible hands, change with the centuries that carry them. And yet this same teacher permitted a stupa to be raised over his remains, and predicted that people would come to it and be gladdened. His followers, who heard him warn against clinging to anything at all, divided his ashes with the solemnity of princes dividing a kingdom, and carried them across a continent.

What does it mean, then, to venerate what remains of a teacher who taught impermanence? Is relic devotion a betrayal of his insight, a concession to human weakness that he tolerated but never shared? Or does it belong, rightly understood, inside the teaching itself — a way of remembering that the Dhamma was not discovered in abstraction but lived, step by step, in a particular human life, by a man who hungered and tired and grew old and died, and who in that very dying showed his disciples how to let go? The sections that follow take those questions slowly, through story, history and reflection. We begin where the relics begin: on the last night of the Buddha.

## 2. The Night at Kushinagar

The fullest early account of the Buddha death comes to us in the Mahaparinibbana Sutta, the long discourse on the great passing, preserved in the Pali canon and treasured in parallel forms in other early collections. It is not a biography in the modern sense. It is a liturgical memory, shaped by generations of recitation, and it moves with a deliberate, almost processional pace through the last months of a long life: the final journey northward, the stops at villages and groves, the teachings given when strength was already failing, and at last the arrival at Kushinagar, a modest town of the Mallas, in what is now Uttar Pradesh. To read it attentively, even in paraphrase, is to feel the ground shifting under the first community. The teacher who had walked the roads of the Ganges plain for forty-five years of ministry was about to leave them, and everyone in the story knows it.

The narrative prepares for the end long before it arrives. At the Capala shrine near Vaishali, the Buddha gives Ananda, his devoted attendant and cousin, an opportunity that passes unrecognised — a hint that a Buddha, if asked, might extend his life for the remainder of an aeon. Ananda, distracted or overcome, does not ask, and the moment closes. Later the Buddha announces, with a calm that still startles readers, that in three months the final passing will take place. The earth, in the telling, trembles. Ananda begs him to remain. The answer is gentle and immovable: everything that is born must die, and he has already laid down the condition. The point the tradition wants us to absorb is not fatalism. It is that even a Buddha does not exempt his own body from the law he discovered. His enlightenment is not a magic that suspends impermanence. It is a complete understanding of impermanence, lived without flinching to the end.

The journey continues through illness. At Beluva, during the rains retreat, a severe sickness seizes him, and he recovers, the text suggests, largely because his work of farewell is not yet done and Ananda has not yet heard what he needs to hear. At Pava he accepts a last meal from Cunda the smith — a dish whose exact nature scholars still discuss — and a painful dysentery follows. Even then the dying man thinks of the giver, sending word that Cunda should bear no remorse, that the last meal before the final passing and the first meal before the enlightenment stand as offerings of equal and immense merit. It is a characteristic gesture. In extremity, his attention moves outward, to protect a humble artisan from a lifetime of guilt.

Between twin sal trees at Kushinagar, in the grove of the Mallas, a couch is prepared with its head to the north, and the Buddha lies down on his right side, one foot resting on the other, mindful and clearly comprehending. The trees, out of season, shower blossoms. Ananda weeps behind the lodging, leaning on the doorpost, confessing that he is still a learner and that his teacher is passing before his own work is complete. The Buddha calls him back and comforts him with a teaching that reaches far beyond one grieving attendant. Be islands unto yourselves, he tells the monks in the discourses of this final period; be a refuge unto yourselves, with the Dhamma as your island and the Dhamma as your refuge, and no other refuge. The phrasing varies across retellings, but the burden is constant. When the person is gone, the practice must become the shelter.

Most important for our subject is the instruction about succession. Ananda had wondered, perhaps aloud in the community, who would lead when the teacher was gone. The Buddha refuses to appoint a successor. Instead, in one of the most consequential sentences in Buddhist history, he declares that the Dhamma and the Vinaya — the teaching and the discipline he has set out — will be the teacher after his passing. No abbot, no patriarch, no relic even, is installed in his place. Authority migrates from a person to a path: to truths that can be tested in experience and to a communal discipline that can hold those who test them. Every later development in Buddhism, including the veneration of relics, has to be measured against that refusal. Whatever a stupa is, it is not a throne. Whatever a relic does, it does not rule.

And yet the same discourse contains the other instruction, the one that gives this article its subject. Ananda asks how the body should be treated. The first answer is almost a dismissal: the monks should not trouble themselves with the honours of a Tathagata; they should strive for their own liberation, and there are wise householders and rulers who will see to the honours. Then, pressed for what those honours should be, the Buddha describes the treatment given to a cakravartin, a wheel-turning universal monarch. The body is to be wrapped in layers of new cloth and carded cotton, placed in an iron vessel with oils and fragrant substances, cremated on a pyre of perfumes, and at a crossroads a stupa is to be raised for him. There, the discourse says, people will bring garlands, scents and colours, will pay homage and will calm and gladden their minds, and that gladness will bear fruit for them for a long time.

The passage is remarkable for its balance. Four places, the Buddha tells Ananda, are worthy of pilgrimage and will inspire a sense of urgency and reverence in the faithful: the place of his birth at Lumbini, the place of his enlightenment at Bodh Gaya, the place of his first turning of the wheel of Dhamma at Sarnath, and the place of his final passing at Kushinagar. Pilgrimage, in other words, is authorised by the founder himself, not invented by later sentiment. But its purpose is always stated in terms of the pilgrim inner state — reverence that ripens into effort, memory that ripens into practice. The site is a support for the mind, never a substitute for the work only the mind can do.

That night, the tradition tells us, the Buddha gave his final teaching to a last convert, the wanderer Subhadda, who had come with questions and was at first turned away by a protective Ananda, then admitted by the dying teacher himself. Near the end he addressed the assembled monks and asked, three times, whether any of them harboured a doubt or perplexity about the Buddha, the Dhamma or the Sangha, urging them to speak now and not regret later that they had stayed silent while they stood face to face with the teacher. Silence answered him. Then came the last words, remembered across the Buddhist world in many languages, whose sense is this: all conditioned things are subject to decay; strive on with diligence. He passed through the meditative absorptions in sequence, forward and back, and entered final nirvana, never to be reborn.

The Mallas of Kushinagar honoured the body for days with dance, song, music, garlands and perfumed cloth, as the discourse describes, before the cremation at the Makutabandhana shrine. When the pyre had burned and been cooled with scented water, what remained — bone fragments, teeth, ashes — became the object of an attention that has never since entirely faded. The monks, following their instruction, did not scramble for the remains. The lay world did something else. Kings and clans sent messengers. A crisis gathered over the ashes of a man who owned nothing. And a brahmin named Dona stepped forward to solve it, in a scene the tradition has retold ever since as a model of how reverence can be shared instead of fought over.

## 3. Dona and the Eight Shares

The story of the division of the relics, told at the close of the Mahaparinibbana Sutta and elaborated in later commentaries and chronicles, begins in tension sharp enough to be dangerous. When news spread that the Buddha had died at Kushinagar and that the Mallas held his cremated remains, claimants arrived from every direction. Ajatashatru, the powerful king of Magadha, sent his demand from Rajagriha. The Licchavis of Vaishali came. The Sakyas of Kapilavastu came — the Buddha own clan, who could plead the kinship of blood. The Bulis of Allakappa, the Koliyas of Ramagrama, a brahmin community from Vethadipa, and the Mallas of Pava all pressed their claims, alongside the Mallas of Kushinagar who held the relics and at first refused to surrender any portion, declaring that the teacher had died in their territory and the honour was theirs alone.

Each claim, considered on its own, had force. Magadha had power and had sheltered the Buddha through many rains retreats; Bimbisara and Ajatashatru, for all the latter terrible history, had been royal patrons. The Sakyas had given him his birth and his youth. Vaishali he had loved and visited repeatedly; it was near Vaishali that he had allowed the founding of the order of nuns and announced his approaching death. The list of claimants reads like a map of his life. Every city on it could point to a grove, a discourse, a rains season, a conversion. The relics were contested precisely because the teaching had been given so widely and received so deeply. Had he taught in one place only, there would have been one shrine and no dispute. Because he belonged, in memory, to them all, they all reached for what was left of him.

Armies, the later tellings suggest, were not far behind the messengers. The scene has an uncomfortable familiarity: grief hardening into entitlement, reverence curdling into rivalry, the remains of a teacher of non-attachment becoming a cause of potential war. Into this standoff stepped Dona, a brahmin known for learning and for a reputation for fairness, who is said to have climbed to a vantage point and addressed the assembled parties in verse. The wording is paraphrased here, as everywhere in this article, but its argument survives every translation. The Buddha, Dona reminded them, had taught forbearance all his life. It would be a disgrace for strife, wounds and battle to arise over the division of the remains of the best of beings. Let them instead unite, in concord and friendship, and divide the relics into eight equal shares, so that stupas might rise in many places and the many people who had devotion might have their eyes gladdened across the whole land.

The argument worked because it did three things at once. It appealed to the teacher example rather than to any claimant strength. It reframed division not as loss but as multiplication — eight shrines would serve the Dhamma better than one hoard. And it offered every party an honourable exit, equal in dignity to every other. The claimants, to their credit in the story, recognised the fairness of the proposal and entrusted the division to Dona himself. Tradition adds a pleasing detail: they told him to keep a share for himself as well, and he, instead of claiming a portion of bone, took the vessel with which he had measured out the shares, and raised a stupa over that. Even the measuring tool became sacred by association — an early hint of the widening categories of relic that a later section will explore.

So the relics were divided into eight portions. The recipients, as the early account lists them, were Ajatashatru of Magadha, who enshrined his share at Rajagriha; the Licchavis of Vaishali; the Sakyas of Kapilavastu; the Bulis of Allakappa; the Koliyas of Ramagrama; the brahmin of Vethadipa; the Mallas of Pava; and the Mallas of Kushinagar, whose initial refusal ended in their accepting one share among equals — a conclusion the story clearly regards as their education. Over each portion a great stupa was raised. These eight original stupas, the garbha stupas of the tradition, stand at the head of a family that would eventually number in the tens of thousands, from the monumental brick mounds of the Ganges plain to the whitewashed chortens of Himalayan passes and the gilded spires of Southeast Asia.

Then come the latecomers, and with them the story most tender coda. The Moriyas of Pipphalivana arrived too late; the division was complete. Rather than send them away empty-handed or reopen the settlement, the assembly gave them the embers of the cremation pyre — the charcoal and ash that remained. The Moriyas accepted the gift without resentment and raised a stupa over the embers in their own country. A further tradition adds a stupa for the urn itself, completing a set of ten early stupas: eight of the corporeal relics, one of the embers, one of the vessel. The Piprahwa discoveries discussed later in this article take their name and much of their fascination from this same border country of clans and small republics, where memory, archaeology and devotion have been negotiating with one another for more than a century.

What does the episode mean, beyond its value as a founding story? First, it establishes the pattern that governs relic history at its best: the relic is for sharing. Again and again in the centuries that follow — in Ashoka redistribution, in the carrying of relics to Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, in the modern exposition that brings relics from a museum in Delhi to a pagoda in Namsai — the movement is outward, centrifugal, a deliberate dispersal of presence so that more people may stand in its light. Hoarding, by contrast, is always the story failure mode, the Kushinagar Mallas first instinct, corrected by counsel.

Second, Dona mediation is remembered because it models a distinctively Buddhist peacemaking. He does not split the difference between armed camps in the manner of a merely political compromise, though the outcome looks like one. He changes the question. The parties had been asking who deserved the relics most. Dona asks what the relics are for. If their purpose is to gladden and inspire the many, then the answer follows by itself: let there be many shrines. Much Buddhist social teaching since has worked in exactly this way, returning a dispute about possession to a reflection about purpose.

Third, the story quietly democratises the sacred. A cakravartin relics would have been a royal monopoly, guarded in a capital. The Buddha relics, by the logic of the eight shares, belong also to small republics, to a brahmin village, to late-arriving Moriyas content with embers. The embers stupa may be the most moving of the ten. It declares that even the trace of a trace, received with gratitude, is enough to build a life of practice around. Generations of devotees who would never see a major relic — who would bow instead before an image, a tree grown from a cutting, a copied page, a handful of ash — are already present, in promise, in the Moriyas walking home with their humble portion and their heads high.

None of this requires us to treat every detail of the account as a court transcript. The Mahaparinibbana Sutta reached its present form through generations of communal recitation, and historians debate the layers within it as they debate most early texts. But the tradition memory is coherent, early and remarkably consistent on the points that matter here: cremation, contention, mediation, eight shares, stupas. And the archaeological landscape lends it a broad plausibility — ancient stupas do stand, or stood, at or near most of the places the story names, their cores dating in some cases to very early periods beneath later enlargements. Story and soil, in this instance, point the same way. Within a few generations, however, the eightfold map would be redrawn by an emperor whose devotion and power together scattered the relics further than Dona could have imagined.


## 4. Ashoka and the Widening Circle

Every account of how Buddhism became a world tradition passes, sooner or later, through the figure of Ashoka, the Mauryan emperor who ruled much of the Indian subcontinent in the third century before the common era. His edicts, carved on pillars and rocks from Afghanistan to Karnataka, survive as hard historical evidence of a ruler commending tolerance, welfare, medical care for humans and animals, and respect for parents, teachers and servants of every sect. Around that solid core, Buddhist memory built a vast edifice of story — the Ashokavadana and the Divyavadana among the Sanskrit sources, the Sri Lankan chronicles among the Pali — and it is inside that edifice, presented explicitly here as tradition and legend rather than verified history, that the great redistribution of the Buddha relics belongs.

The legend, in its best-known form, tells that Ashoka, converted to Buddhism and grieved by the violence of his early conquests, above all the conquest of Kalinga, resolved to honour the Buddha by opening seven of the eight original relic stupas and redistributing their contents across his empire. The eighth stupa, at Ramagrama, guarded in the story by naga serpents who worshipped the relics themselves, resisted him; the nagas pleaded, or the signs warned, and the emperor desisted, leaving that one deposit undisturbed. From the seven he recovered, the legend says, he divided the relics into eighty-four thousand portions and built eighty-four thousand stupas over them in a single day, the work coordinated by the elder Yasa and accomplished with the help of yakshas who raised the monuments simultaneously across the land as the sun moved.

Eighty-four thousand is not a statistic. It is a sacred number in Indian tradition, the conventional count of the teachings of the Buddha, of the sections of the Dhamma, in some reckonings of the very atoms of doctrine. To say Ashoka built eighty-four thousand stupas is to say, in the language of legend, that he placed a relic at the heart of every teaching and a teaching within reach of every subject — that the empire and the Dhamma were mapped onto one another, village by village. The historian will rightly note that no excavation will ever confirm the single day or the simultaneous raising. But the legend encodes a real transformation that archaeology does confirm in outline: between the Mauryan period and the centuries that followed, stupas multiplied enormously, older brick cores at sites like Sanchi, Bharhut, Vaishali and Kushinagar were enlarged in stone, and relic deposits travelled along the routes of trade and pilgrimage into the Deccan, the north-west, and eventually beyond India altogether.

Consider what a relic made possible that a text, in that age, could not easily do. A manuscript required scribes, materials and a community of readers; in a largely non-literate world its reach was real but narrow. A relic required only a story and a mound. Carried into a new valley, enshrined with ceremony, it founded a sacred geography on the spot. The local hill became a place where the Buddha, in the only physical sense now available, was present. Festivals gathered around the stupa. Donors gave land and gold and recorded their gifts in inscriptions that still survive by the thousand — and those inscriptions, at Sanchi and elsewhere, famously name not only kings but monks, nuns, merchants, artisans, housewives and whole guilds, including the ivory-workers of Vidisha who carved one of the great gateways. The relic, in short, was a seed-pearl. Around an almost invisible centre, layer upon layer of communal life accreted: monastery, rest house, tank, fair, market, scriptorium, school.

The metaphor of seed-pearls is worth keeping because it explains the pattern of Buddhism spread across Asia better than any image of conquest or even of preaching alone. To Sri Lanka, tradition carries both a sapling of the Bodhi tree, brought by the nun Sanghamitta, and relics that anchored the great stupas of Anuradhapura; the island chronicles treat the arrival of a Buddha relic as the arrival of the Buddha himself, received by kings with the protocol due to a sovereign. To the kingdoms of Southeast Asia, relics travelled by sea and river, and the great stupa-fields of Burma, the chedis of Thailand, the that of Laos and the temples of Cambodia each claim their deposit and their founding story. To Central Asia and then China, relics moved along the Silk Road; the finger bone of the Buddha enshrined at Famen Temple near Xi an, buried in an underground palace of the Tang dynasty and rediscovered in 1987 in a crypt of gold and silver caskets nested one inside another, shows how far the seed could travel and how lavish the layers around it could grow. To Tibet and the Himalaya came teeth, bones and the self-arisen images and ringsel that a later section will consider. Japan received relics through Korea and China and built its pagodas, direct descendants of the stupa by way of Chinese towers, to house them.

None of this movement was merely decorative. In each new home, the relic performed a delicate negotiation between the universal teaching and the local land. Buddhism did not usually ask a valley to abandon its spirits, its ancestors or its sacred mountain. It set a stupa on the hill, absorbed the local guardian into the retinue of protectors, and let the old stories retell themselves around the new centre. Relic processions — still among the great public festivals of Asia, from the Esala Perahera that honours the Tooth Relic at Kandy to the expositions in Arunachal Pradesh that frame this article — carried the presence out of the shrine and through the streets, blessing fields, markets and neighbourhoods impartially. The sacred, in this dispensation, is not fenced. It circulates.

A clear-eyed account must also admit the shadows. Relics could be captured in war and carried off as trophies, as the Tooth Relic history in Sri Lanka and the struggles over relics between rival Burmese and Thai kingdoms attest. Forgery and multiplication troubled even medieval devotees; where every great temple needed a deposit, supply found ways to meet demand, and the line between pious multiplication and fraud was debated within the tradition itself. Royal patrons used relic enshrinement to sanctify their rule, and the merit they proclaimed was never wholly separable from the legitimacy they gained. The tradition, to its credit, preserved its own antidote in plain sight: the founder instruction that the Dhamma, not any object, is the teacher. A stupa raised by a king could bless his realm or advertise his power, and often did both; what it could not do, on the Buddha own terms, was liberate a single being who did not walk the path. The widening circle widened the invitation. It never widened a shortcut, because there is none.

Modern India has, perhaps unexpectedly, renewed the pattern. The relics preserved in the National Museum in New Delhi — including those from Piprahwa, to which we now turn — have in recent decades travelled on exposition to Sri Lanka, Mongolia, Thailand, and within India to places like Namsai, received by heads of government and by village devotees in the same week. Diplomats speak of soft power and cultural bonds, and they are not wrong; but the queues tell the older story. What Ashoka legend imagined — presence dispersed so widely that no one need live far from a reminder — the aeroplane and the museum vault now accomplish by other means. The circle is still widening. Its centre, however, remains a cremation ground in Kushinagar and a question about what, exactly, the faithful believe is present when they bow.

## 5. Piprahwa

If Dona story is the tradition memory of the relics, Piprahwa is where that memory meets the spade, the inscription and the sometimes uncomfortable discipline of modern scholarship. The site is a large stupa mound near the village of Piprahwa in present-day Siddharthnagar district, Uttar Pradesh, close to the border with Nepal and not far from the ancient Sakya country — the Buddha own homeland. Its modern history begins in January 1898, when William Claxton Peppé, a British estate manager and amateur antiquarian whose land included the mound, excavated into its heart and uncovered a massive stone coffer. Inside lay a collection that still provokes argument: several reliquary vessels, among them a steatite vase bearing an inscription in Brahmi script, together with bone fragments, ashes, and a dazzling deposit of offerings — gold ornaments, stars and flowers of gold and silver, pearls, coral, and hundreds of precious and semi-precious stones.

The inscription on the vase became one of the most debated short texts in Indian epigraphy. Peppé contemporaries, including the great scholar Georg Bühler, read it as a dedication recording that the relics of the Buddha of the Sakyas were enshrined by members of the Sakya community — in some readings, by the brothers and families associated with a named devotee. If that reading were straightforwardly correct, Piprahwa would be nothing less than the Kapilavastu share of the original eightfold division, excavated. But the reading has never been straightforward. Scholars have debated the grammar, the referents of its words, the dating of its letter forms, and whether the text speaks of the Buddha relics themselves or of the relics of the Buddha Sakya kinsmen. Later writers, most forcefully in the late twentieth century, challenged the authenticity of aspects of the discovery and raised the shadow of forgery that has hung, at times unfairly and at times usefully, over many colonial-era excavations. Other specialists have defended the find, re-examined the script, and placed the deposit in the early centuries of stupa worship, whether or not it represents an original share.

The honest summary, and the one this article will stand by, is that scholars debate the readings and the claims, and contested claims must not be asserted as fact. What is not seriously in doubt is that Piprahwa is an ancient and important stupa, repeatedly enlarged, in the Sakya borderland, containing early relic deposits of real significance. What the bone fragments in the Peppé coffer ultimately were — whose, and deposited when — remains a question where devotion, national memory and scholarship pull in different directions, and where a responsible writer distinguishes what is known from what is hoped. That distinction does not weaken the site. If anything, Piprahwa teaches the most useful lesson in this whole subject: reverence and rigour are not enemies. The tradition survived its own internal critics for two thousand years; it can survive epigraphers.

The story resumed, on firmer archaeological ground, in the 1970s. Between 1971 and 1977 the Archaeological Survey of India excavated at Piprahwa under K. M. Srivastava, working deeper into the stupa than the earlier digging had reached. In the earliest mud-brick phase of the monument, at a depth below the Peppé discovery, the team found two soapstone caskets containing, between them, twenty-two bone fragments. The depth and the primitive construction of that earliest stupa layer persuaded Srivastava and many who followed him that here, rather than in the later and richer deposit above, lay the most ancient enshrinement at the site — possibly, in his interpretation, the original Sakya share itself, later supplemented and monumentalised. That interpretation too is argued over, and should be reported as interpretation. The fragments themselves, however, are a matter of record and of custody: of the twenty-two, twenty are kept at the National Museum, New Delhi, and two at the Indian Museum, Kolkata. It is from this reserve, cared for in the capital, that relics have travelled in our own time on the expositions described at the opening of this article, including the present journey through Arunachal Pradesh. When devotees bow at Namsai, the chain of custody they are trusting runs through Srivastava trenches and museum registers as well as through scripture — a very modern form of an ancient reliance on custodians.

The Peppé material had a more scattered fate. The bone relics from the 1898 find were presented, in a famous act of colonial diplomacy, to the King of Siam, the only Buddhist monarch then reigning, and were shared onward to shrines in Burma and Ceylon, so that fragments of Piprahwa rest today in more than one country. The treasure of jewels and gold, by contrast, largely entered the Indian Museum in Kolkata, while a portion — the share allotted under the treasure rules of the time to the excavator family — remained with the Peppé family and passed down through generations, much of it held in private hands abroad. For more than a century that division sat quietly in the footnotes. Then, in 2025, it returned to the headlines: the Piprahwa jewel collection retained by the family was offered for sale at auction in Hong Kong, the Government of India intervened with legal and diplomatic efforts to halt the sale and secure the return of what it regarded as sacred heritage inseparable from the relics themselves, the auction was postponed, and the collection was repatriated to India, where it was received with public ceremony and destined for museum display and, in due course, for exposition alongside the relics it once accompanied.

The 2025 return deserves reflection beyond the satisfaction of a recovered treasure. The jewels are not relics in the strict sense — they are offerings, the flowers that do not fade, laid beside the bone by devotees whose names we will never know. Yet the intensity of the campaign for their return, and the emotion of their homecoming, shows how completely the categories blur in lived devotion. To separate the gems from the fragments permanently, by a sale that would have scattered them among private buyers, struck millions of people — far beyond the community of practising Buddhists — as a kind of dismemberment. Heritage law, post-colonial restitution debates and religious feeling converged on a single intuition: some objects carry a meaning that the market cannot price because the meaning is the relationship, between a teacher, his first followers, and the unbroken line of those who still come to bow.

Piprahwa also, unavoidably, raises the question of who owns the Buddha past. The stupa stood in India; the Buddha homeland straddles the modern India–Nepal border, and Lumbini, his birthplace, lies in Nepal, whose claim on this heritage is profound and legitimate. The Sakyas were not Indian or Nepali in any modern national sense; those categories would have meant nothing to them. The wisest voices in the Piprahwa debates, scholarly and devotional alike, converge on stewardship rather than ownership as the governing idea: excavate carefully, publish honestly, conserve transparently, share generously. An exposition that carries relics from a Delhi vault to Tawang, Bomdila and Namsai is stewardship in motion. So is an inscription left open to rival readings. The past, like the relic in Dona hands, turns out to be something that does most good when it is divided justly and held lightly.


## 6. What a Relic Is

Before going further into devotion and architecture, it helps to ask a plainer question: what, in the Buddhist understanding, is a relic? The vocabulary itself carries the teaching. In Pali and Sanskrit the common term is dhatu, a word whose range runs from element and basic constituent to relic — as if to say that what remains of the Buddha is not a souvenir laid on top of reality but reality at its most elemental, the body resolved back into the constituents of which all bodies are made. Another term, sarira, properly means body, and by extension the small, pearl-like or crystalline remains that tradition reports finding among the ashes of the Buddha and of great saints. The double vocabulary holds the paradox in miniature. A relic is body and it is element; it is personal and it is utterly impersonal; it is his, and it is what everything is.

Classical Buddhist reflection, systematised in the commentaries and in the manuals of pilgrimage, distinguishes three kinds of relic, and the distinctions repay attention because they map the whole geography of Buddhist devotion. The first kind is the corporeal relic, sarira-dhatu in the usual formulation: the actual remains of the body — bone, tooth, hair, nail. The Tooth Relic at Kandy, the hair relics enshrined in the great pagodas of Burma, the bone fragments of Piprahwa and Kushinagar, all belong here. These are the relics of the eight shares and of Ashoka redistribution, the ones over which kings negotiated and for which stupas were first raised. Their power in the imagination is the power of contact unbroken: this was part of him, this lay within the body that sat under the Bodhi tree and walked to Sarnath and lay down at last between the sal trees.

The second kind is the relic of use, sometimes called paribhoga relics: things the Buddha touched, used or occupied, which carry his presence by association. The supreme example is the Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya — not the present tree in any simple botanical sense, but the living succession of that tree, grown from its seed and from the sapling carried to Sri Lanka in the third century before the common era, whose descendant at Anuradhapura is among the oldest historically documented trees on earth, still guarded and watered and garlanded after more than two thousand years. To this class belong the alms bowl, whose legendary journeys through Gandhara, Persia and Sri Lanka filled whole chronicles; the seat of enlightenment, the vajrasana at Bodh Gaya; the robe, the staff, the sites of his footprints, marked with the auspicious signs and venerated across Asia long before anthropomorphic images of the Buddha became common. A relic of use works like the measuring vessel of Dona: it was there, it served him, and its nearness is its sanctity.

The third kind is the commemorative or indicative relic, the uddesika class: images, stupas, inscribed texts and representations made after his passing to point toward him. Strictly, the commentators often treat this class as including the Dhamma itself in written form — a manuscript enshrined in a stupa, a verse on impermanence sealed in a reliquary, the famous practice, widespread in the Buddhist world, of depositing copies of sacred texts or of the dependent-origination formula inside images and miniature stupas as their inner relic, their dhamma-body. Here the tradition makes its most profound move. The Buddha physical body is gone; his body of teaching remains, and it can be enshrined with exactly the same rites. A book can be a relic. A remembered sentence, carried in the mind and acted on, is the relic at work.

Within the Tibetan and Himalayan world, one further phenomenon is spoken of with particular tenderness: ringsel. The word denotes the small, pearl-like, sometimes multicoloured beads that devotees report finding among the cremated remains of realised masters, or occasionally appearing, in the accounts of the faithful, in connection with statues, texts and the remains of earlier saints. Tradition regards ringsel as the crystallised essence of a life of compassion and wisdom, and stories tell of them multiplying in the presence of sincere devotion. This article describes ringsel as the tradition belief, respectfully and in its own terms: it is what Himalayan Buddhists, including the communities of Tawang through which the present exposition has passed, understand themselves to be honouring. It does not assert miracles as laboratory fact, and it does not need to. The anthropologist can study the reports; the chemist can study the beads; the devotee bows to what they signify — that a human life, fully given to awakening, leaves something behind that is not exhausted by the word ash. Between mockery and credulity there is a third stance, and it is the stance of this whole series: report the belief faithfully, honour the people who hold it, and keep the categories clear.

Clarity about categories also protects against a misunderstanding that has dogged relic veneration from outside, and sometimes from inside, for centuries: the idea that Buddhists worship bone. The classical theory, stated in the commentaries and lived in the practice of most devotees one actually meets, is subtler. The relic is a support for recollection, a nimitta, a sign that gathers the mind. Honour offered to the relic is understood to reach, through it, the qualities the Buddha embodied and taught — awakening, compassion, fearlessness, peace — in much the way that honour offered to a flag reaches a nation, or to a portrait reaches the person portrayed, except that here the person portrayed has explicitly redirected all honour onward to the teaching. Where veneration forgets that redirection, Buddhist teachers themselves have always been its sharpest critics, from the early discourses onward. Where veneration remembers it, the relic functions exactly as the founder pilgrimage teaching said it should: the mind is gladdened, calmed and stirred to effort.

There is one more distinction, quieter than the rest, that the modern exposition circuit makes visible. Some relics live permanently in one shrine and receive the world; the Tooth at Kandy is the great example, the still centre of a nation pilgrimage. Others travel and visit the world: the Piprahwa fragments in their museum custody, the relics of the Buddha and his chief disciples that tour from country to country, received in stadiums and pagodas by crowds who may number in the millions across a single exposition. Both patterns are ancient — the Buddha himself, after all, was a traveller who owned nothing and went to the people — and both answer real needs. The fixed shrine teaches stability, the long fidelity of a place. The travelling relic teaches that presence is not diminished by being shared, any more than a lamp is diminished by lighting another lamp, in the image the tradition loves. At Namsai this month, the travelling kind has come to rest for a week in a golden house built by the faithful of the plains. To understand why hands fold and eyes fill when the reliquary is unveiled, we have to look not at the bone, but at the heart that beholds it.

## 7. Why the Hand Reaches Out

Watch a queue at a relic exposition for an hour, and you will learn more about the psychology of devotion than a shelf of theories can teach. A grandmother lifts a child to see. A young soldier on leave bows with the precision of parade and then, unexpectedly, weeps. A group of schoolchildren, noisy in the approach, fall silent at the threshold without being told. A monk who has presumably seen relics a hundred times prostrates as if for the first time. Nobody in that queue believes, if you ask them carefully, that a bone fragment thinks, hears or grants favours like a clerk dispensing permits. Ask what they are doing, and the answers converge on a small set of human words: remembering, thanking, being near, making a resolution, praying for a mother, asking for strength to forgive. The hand reaches out — toward the glass, toward the flowers, toward the ground in prostration — because the heart has already reached, and the body insists on joining it.

Buddhist practice has a precise name for the core of this act: buddhanussati, the recollection of the Buddha. It stands first in the classical lists of recollections, and its method is not petition but contemplation. The meditator brings to mind the qualities of the Tathagata — his worthiness, his perfect awakening, his knowledge and conduct, his happy attainment, his knowledge of the world, his incomparable guidance of those ready to be guided, his role as teacher of gods and humans, his blessedness — and lets the mind steep in those qualities until, as the early texts describe, it becomes glad, calm and concentrated, its hindrances temporarily set aside. Relic veneration, rightly understood, is buddhanussati with the eyes open and the body involved. The reliquary does for the scattered layperson, in a crowded hall, on a working day, what the meditation cushion does for the monk at dawn: it gives recollection an object vivid enough to hold a wandering mind.

Memory is the first mechanism, and the most underestimated. Human beings do not remember abstractions well; we remember places, faces, objects, anniversaries. The whole architecture of Buddhist devotion — the pilgrimage sites, the festivals on the full-moon days of the enlightenment and the passing, the stupas on the hilltops — is a technology of memory built by a tradition that understood, long before modern psychology named it, that what is not recalled is soon not practised. A relic is memory made durable. Texts can be forgotten in a generation of neglect, as nearly happened to Buddhism in the land of its birth; a mound on a hill, tended by a village, outlasts dynasties of neglect and asks its question of every passer-by: who was he, that they built this for him? Many a revival, including the modern recovery of the Buddhist sites of India in the nineteenth century, began with exactly that question asked before a ruin.

Gratitude is the second mechanism, and it gives relic devotion its distinctive emotional warmth. In the Buddhist account of existence, the Buddha is not a creator to be thanked for the world, but a discoverer to be thanked for the map. He found, the tradition says, the ancient path that had been lost, cleared it, and left directions so that others need not wander as long as he did. Gratitude to a benefactor of that magnitude seeks expression beyond words; it wants to give something, carry something, build something, walk somewhere. The flowers that wilt by evening, the gold leaf pressed onto a shrine, the eighty-four thousand stupas of legend, the jewelled offerings buried at Piprahwa by hands unknown — all are gratitude in material form, deliberately disproportionate, because proportion is not the language gratitude speaks. The economist sees waste; the devotee sees the only adequate grammar for a debt that cannot be repaid, only acknowledged and passed on.

Presence is the third and most delicate mechanism, and the one this article title invokes. Presence, here, does not require a metaphysical claim that the Buddha survives as a person inside his relics; orthodox teaching, with its doctrine of final nirvana beyond rebirth, would resist that claim in its crude form, and the many sophisticated ways Mahayana and Vajrayana thought speak of the Buddha continuing presence — through the dharma-body, through blessing, through the sambhogakaya — belong to a fuller treatment than a single section allows. What can be said plainly is phenomenological: in the presence of the relic, devotees experience the teaching as present — not as information, but as encounter. The distance of twenty-five centuries collapses. He was real; he stood no taller than a man; he ate, he taught, he died, and this touched that life. For a faith whose central claims are about what a human being can become, that collapse of distance is not a distraction from the doctrine. It is the doctrine, felt in the body: if awakening happened once, in a body like this, it is not a fairy tale about someone else.

Against all this stands the tradition own warning, which honest exposition must never omit. Attachment is the root of suffering, and attachment to relics is not exempt. The discourses are unsparing about grasping at rites and objects as if they could save by themselves, and the history surveyed in this article — relics fought over, captured, forged, sold — shows the warning was needed. The line between veneration and grasping runs through every devotee heart, and the tradition locates the test in the fruit: veneration that sends a person home kinder, steadier and more diligent in practice has done its work; grasping that sends a person home prouder of possessing, nearer to contempt for those without access, or lazier in the real work of transformation, has failed it, however correct its outward forms. The relic, in the end, is a mirror. It returns to each visitor the quality of the attention they brought.

Perhaps that is why the exposition at a place like Namsai matters beyond its week of darshan. Most of those who bow there will never read the Mahaparinibbana Sutta, never hear of Dona or debate the Piprahwa inscription. But they will remember, on some later night of difficulty, that they once stood in silence before what remains of the Buddha and felt their minds grow calm and glad — precisely the fruit the founder predicted for those who visit the sacred places. Some will take up a practice. Some will simply be a little less harsh, a little less afraid. The hand that reaches out toward the reliquary is, if the teaching is true, the same hand that must eventually open and let go of everything, including the relic, including the reaching. Veneration is the reaching. Wisdom is the opening. The tradition has always insisted that a complete life of practice needs both gestures, in their season — and that the building which houses the reaching, the stupa itself, has been preaching that double lesson in stone since before the first image of the Buddha was ever carved.

## 8. The Stupa as a Book in Stone

Stand before the great stupa at Sanchi at dawn, as generations of travellers have done, and the first impression is of simplicity carried to the point of majesty. A vast hemispherical mound of brick and stone rises from a circular terrace, crowned by a small railed enclosure and a triple umbrella of stone. There is, in the earliest stupas, no image at all — no face, no figure, nothing to meet the eye with an eye. And yet no one who walks the path around it mistakes it for a mere tomb. The stupa is a diagram of the cosmos, a portrait of the Buddha in the language of geometry, and a book whose pages are walked rather than turned. To read it, we need its vocabulary, which the centuries of builders from Ashoka age to the Golden Pagoda at Namsai have kept remarkably stable.

The great mound itself is the anda, the egg. The name is exact: a sealed, fertile roundness, containing the relic at its heart as an egg contains life. In its ancestry lie the burial mounds of ancient India and, behind them, the cosmic egg of still older imagination; in its Buddhist meaning, the anda is both the Buddha body enshrined and the world-mountain, the axis around which existence turns. Solid throughout — the early stupas are not hollow chambers but masses of brick and earth around a tiny central deposit — it preaches by its very construction the teaching of the relic itself: the precious thing is small, hidden and central, and everything visible exists to protect and proclaim it. Empires of masonry for a fragment of bone. The disproportion is the doctrine.

Above the dome sits the harmika, the square railed enclosure at the summit, representing the sacred precinct above the world — in older interpretation, the heaven of the gods, in the reading of the faithful, the goal beyond the round of rebirth. From its centre rises the shaft bearing the chattra, the umbrella discs, usually three, which proclaim sovereignty and protection: the relic beneath is no pauper remains but a universal monarch of the spirit, entitled to the royal umbrella the founder own instruction had foreseen when he directed that his body be honoured as a cakravartin body. Around the base, at ground level and on the terrace, runs the path of pradaksina, circumambulation, the ritual walking of the monument with the right side — the side of honour — always toward it. The devotee does not, in the ancient pattern, enter the stupa at all. There is no interior to enter. One walks around the presence, slowly, clockwise as the sun moves, letting the body learn what the mind is still debating: that a life can have a centre, and that practice is the art of keeping that centre on one right hand through every step of the day.

At the cardinal points of the developed stupa stand the toranas, the great gateways, and it is here that the book opens into narrative. The toranas of Sanchi, carved in the century or so around the turn of the common era by generations of donors whose names crowd the inscriptions, and the earlier railing medallions of Bharhut, now largely in the Indian Museum at Kolkata, carry the most exuberant storytelling in early Indian art: the previous lives of the Buddha in the Jataka tales, his final life from descent and birth to enlightenment and first sermon, crowds and kings and animals and gods, all rendered in stone of inexhaustible invention — and, famously, without the Buddha himself shown in human form. In the early art he is present by sign: the empty throne, the Bodhi tree, the wheel, the footprint, the stupa itself. Aniconic, scholars call it, and debate its reasons; but its devotional effect is unmistakable and entirely in keeping with everything this article has traced. The teacher cannot be depicted because the teacher is the path; the signs point, as the relic points, as the Dhamma points, beyond themselves.

Bharhut and Sanchi also teach us who built this book. Their donative inscriptions, hundreds of them, record a whole society at devotion: monks and nuns in striking numbers, merchants and bankers, artisans and their guilds, royal scribes, housewives identified by their villages, donors from towns across central India and the Deccan. The stupa was not a royal vanity project with a public relations inscription. It was the closest thing the ancient Buddhist world had to a universal public institution — part shrine, part festival ground, part bank of merit, part library in pictures for those who could not read letters. A Jataka carved on a railing taught the illiterate farmer the story of the Buddha generosity as a hare, or his patience as a monkey king, while his children traced the animals with their fingers. Narrative teaching is not a modern adaptation of the stupa idea. It is the stupa idea, present from the first enlargements.

From India the form travelled and translated itself, as the relics did. In Gandhara it grew tall on stepped bases and filled its niches with images. In Nepal it became the great white domes of Swayambhu and Boudhanath, their harmikas painted with the all-seeing eyes that watch over the Kathmandu valley and a thousand Himalayan paths. In Tibet it became the chorten, in eight classical forms commemorating the great events of the Buddha life, lining the approaches to monasteries and the passes where travellers most need blessing. In Burma it multiplied into fields of golden bells at Shwedagon and Bagan; in Thailand it sharpened into the chedi; in Java it rose as Borobudur, the supreme development of the walking book, where the pilgrim circumambulates ascending terraces carved with the whole path from desire to formlessness and emerges at the open summit among perforated stupas and the sky. And in our own framing story it stands as the Golden Pagoda at Namsai, its Burmese tiers naturalised in an Assam-border landscape — proof that the book is still being copied, in new dialects of gold and concrete, by communities who intend to be read by their grandchildren.

What, finally, does the stupa say, taken as a whole? It says that the Buddha is present in the landscape, not locked in a text or a heaven: a farmer can see his reminder from the field, a traveller from the road, a child from the courtyard. It says that presence is approached by walking, not by seizing — the path goes around, and around again, and the centre is never broken open by the devotee hands. It says that a community is healthiest when its best energies gather around a centre that asks nothing but remembrance and gives nothing but orientation. And it says, in its solid silence, the first and last word of the teaching it enshrines: the mound will weather, the gateways will fall and be reassembled by archaeologists, the gold will be renewed and will dull again, and even the relic at the heart of it is a conditioned thing, subject to decay — while the Dhamma it points to, the truth of impermanence and the path out of suffering, stands exactly as available to the visitor of the next century as to the builders of the first. The book in stone is written, deliberately, in a perishable material, about the one thing its author held to be imperishable. That is not a contradiction. It is the whole sermon, and the queue at Namsai, filing past a golden descendant of the first mound at Kushinagar, is its congregation still.

## 9. Anicca at the Reliquary — Impermanence as the Relic's Deepest Sermon

Stand before a reliquary long enough and the teaching begins to change its register. At first the eye is drawn to what has been made to last: gold worked by patient hands, crystal cut to hold light, silk, silver, a casket set inside a casket as if care itself could slow time. Then attention settles, almost reluctantly, on what lies within. A fragment. A grain. Something so small and so plain that, without its setting, a passerby might not notice it at all.

That contrast is not an accident to be explained away. It is the sermon.

The Buddha taught impermanence, anicca, not as a melancholy observation to be agreed with at a distance, but as a truth to be seen in one's own body, breath, feelings and thoughts until seeing loosened the grip of clinging. He spoke of all conditioned things as arising and passing, of the body as subject to ageing, illness and death, of even the most refined states of meditation as not to be taken as self. What gives the relic its peculiar power is that he did not exempt himself from that teaching. The body that had walked the roads of the Gangetic plain, that had sat through the night at Bodh Gaya, that had taught for forty-five years, was allowed to grow old, to sicken, to stop eating, to lie down between two sal trees at Kushinagar and not rise again.

A tradition that wished only to glorify its founder might have softened that ending. The early texts do not. They linger on the failing strength, on Ananda's grief, on the Buddha's own gentle insistence that his disciples not be negligent but strive on with diligence. The last instruction, as paraphrased from the Mahaparinibbana Sutta, is not a promise that the teacher will remain in some hidden form to do the work for them. It is a handing over. The Dhamma will be the teacher after he is gone. Each person is to be an island and a refuge to themselves, with the Dhamma as that refuge, and no other refuge sought outside the practice itself.

To contemplate a relic in that light is therefore a very different act from contemplating a trophy. A trophy says that something has been won and secured against time. A relic says the opposite. Here, it says, is what remains when a human life, even the most awakened human life known to this tradition, has followed the law that governs all lives. The gold does not deny that law. The crystal does not reverse it. They honour what has passed precisely by not pretending that it has not passed.

This is why the practice of maranasati, the recollection of death, belongs so naturally beside the reliquary, and why it needs to be handled gently. Maranasati in the Buddhist tradition is not a cultivation of dread. The texts commend reflecting that death may come this day, this night, before the next meal or the next breath, not to frighten the mind into paralysis but to clarify what matters while there is still time to do it. A person who remembers, calmly and repeatedly, that this body too will be given back to the elements tends to waste less of it. Anger held overnight begins to look expensive. A kindness postponed begins to look like a kindness risked. Generosity delayed until some more convenient season begins to look uncertain, because the season itself is uncertain.

Before a relic, that reflection acquires a face. It is one thing to recite that all beings are subject to death. It is another to stand before the physical remainder of a particular being, whose name one knows, whose words one has read that morning, whose compassion one claims to admire, and to allow the simple inference to land: if this happened to him, in this ordinary biological way, it will happen to me. Not as punishment. Not as failure. As nature.

Handled well, that thought does not make the shrine room sombre in a heavy way. Devotees who have spent time in such places often remark on a lightness there, a quiet that is closer to relief than to gloom. Part of that relief may come from honesty. Much of human suffering is compounded by a private, unspoken demand that the things we love should be exempt from change — that parents should not age, that health should not falter, that a teacher should not die, that we ourselves should somehow be the exception. The relic refuses to collude with that demand, and it refuses kindly. It does not mock the wish. It simply shows, in material form, what the teaching has always said in words.

There is a second, subtler lesson in the smallness of what remains. A human life of eighty years — of teaching, travelling, debating, comforting, admonishing, laughing with disciples, enduring slander and illness and the slow losses of old age — comes down, in physical terms, to this. The disproportion is instructive. What endures of the Buddha in the world is overwhelmingly not bone. It is a way of seeing, preserved in texts and in living practice, re-enacted whenever someone sits down to watch the breath honestly or chooses restraint where indulgence was available or forgives where resentment had taken root. The fragment in the casket is a pointer to that disproportion. It keeps the proportions right. Matter mattered — the Buddha never taught contempt for the body — but matter was never the main thing.

Parents sometimes bring children to a relic exposition and worry, reasonably, about how to speak of death in that setting. The tradition offers a gentler vocabulary than our euphemisms and a more honest one than our bluntest terms. One can say that the Buddha's body grew tired and stopped, as all bodies do; that the people who loved him were sad, as we are sad; that they kept something small to remember him by, as families keep a letter or a photograph; and that remembering him well means trying to live as he taught. A child understands keeping something to remember a grandparent by. The theology can wait. The tenderness and the truthfulness are already the practice.

For the adult practitioner, the reliquary can become a place to make a quiet examination. What am I treating as permanent that is not? A role at work, a reputation, a grievance that has hardened into identity, a version of a loved one frozen at an earlier age, a version of oneself? Anicca is not only a doctrine about the distant future and the final breath. It describes the present texture of things: moods passing, sensations shifting, plans dissolving and reforming, relationships changing their shape even when love remains. To see that clearly is not to love less. It is, paradoxically, to love with less grasping, which is to love with less fear mixed in.

The Buddha's relics, then, preach even when no monk is speaking and no text is open. They preach by being remains. Every other sermon he gave can be quoted, debated, memorised and systematised. This one is simply shown. A small, silent, material fact, set in gold by people who understood that the gold was for the living — for their reverence, their craft, their need to mark importance — and that the fragment itself needed none of it. He had already let go of that body. The question the reliquary leaves with the visitor is whether the visitor is learning, in smaller and daily ways, to let go of anything at all.

## 10. The Body That Became a Teaching

It helps, before speaking of presence after death, to remember how presence worked while the Buddha lived, because the contrast explains both the devotion and the discipline that surrounds the relics.

He was, by all the accounts the tradition has preserved, a teacher who was sought out in person. People travelled. Kings sent messengers and then came themselves. Brahmins walked to debate him, sometimes intending to defeat him in argument and leaving as disciples. Villagers brought questions about crops, marriages, grief and ghosts. The sick were carried into his vicinity. The texts return again and again to the simple geography of it: a person hears that the Blessed One is staying in such a grove or such a park outside such a town, and sets out. The journey itself becomes part of the teaching story — the distance measures the seriousness of the question.

What did they find when they arrived? Not, if the texts are to be trusted, a performance of remoteness. The Buddha is depicted as accessible in a way that repeatedly surprises the expectations of visitors who arrive prepared for either a wonder-worker or an aloof ascetic. He asks after people's welfare. He adjusts his teaching to the listener in front of him, a quality the tradition praises as his skill in means. He eats what is offered. He walks on almsround. He gets dust on his feet. The charisma the texts describe is inseparable from this ordinariness: a man fully present in a body, attending to the particular person before him, neither hurrying the encounter nor inflating it.

One episode, paraphrased from the Mahavagga of the Vinaya, has become the tradition's own commentary on what his physical presence was for. The Buddha came upon a monk who was ill with dysentery, lying neglected in his own filth because the companion who shared his dwelling had abandoned him as useless. The Buddha, with Ananda's help, washed the monk, lifted him, laid him on a bed, and then gathered the community and rebuked them: they had no mother or father to care for them in this life of renunciation, and so they must care for one another; if they did not, who would? The teaching he drew from it, in paraphrase, was that whoever would wait upon the Buddha should wait upon the sick. The equivalence is deliberate and startling. Service to the teacher's body and service to a suffering body are placed on the same ledger, and then the second is quietly given priority, because the teacher was standing there healthy enough to do the washing himself.

Hold that episode in mind at a reliquary and it disciplines the devotion in a healthy way. Presence, in the Buddha's own handling of it, was never an end in itself. It was for the sake of what presence makes possible: instruction received, suffering relieved, a community knit together by mutual care. When devotees today say that they feel the Buddha's presence near his relics, the tradition need not rush either to endorse the claim as metaphysics or to dismiss it as fantasy. It can ask the more useful question the Mahavagga episode suggests: presence for what? If standing near the relic sends a person back into their life more attentive to the sick, the elderly, the neglected person in their own household or ward or street, then the presence has functioned exactly as it functioned when the Buddha knelt with a basin of water. If it sends them away only with a photograph and a sense of spiritual acquisition, something has been missed, and missed in a way the founder himself anticipated and corrected.

The texts also preserve, with unusual candour, the other side of presence: its limits, and the Buddha's refusal to let people lean on it. Disciples who wanted to remain constantly near him were sometimes sent away to practise alone. The monk Vakkali, who longed simply to gaze at the Buddha's physical form, was gently but firmly redirected, in the well-known paraphrase of that teaching: seeing the body is not the point; whoever sees the Dhamma sees the Buddha, and whoever sees the Buddha sees the Dhamma. The form, the rupa, beautiful and revered as it was, was declared insufficient while he was still alive to declare it. That saying does a great deal of quiet work in relic theology. It means the tradition was warned, by its founder and in advance, against mistaking the remains of the form for the reality the form had served.

After the parinirvana, then, presence continues, but differently, and the tradition has generally known it. It continues as memory organised by places and objects — the stupas at the sites of the birth, the awakening, the first teaching and the passing, and the relics that multiplied those sites across Asia. It continues as text, recited and studied. It continues as lineage, teacher to student, in unbroken chains of ordination and instruction that communities guard carefully. And it continues, Buddhists would say, as efficacy: the Dhamma still works when practised, grief still softens under impermanence contemplated honestly, greed still loosens under generosity practised repeatedly. None of these modes requires the claim that the Buddha is somehow contained in the casket. All of them explain why people weep at the casket anyway. Love and gratitude do not consult theology before they rise. They rise where memory is concentrated, and a relic concentrates memory as few things can.

There is also a communal dimension that the living presence created and the relics inherited. To travel to see the Buddha was rarely a solitary act for long; one joined a road full of others travelling for the same reason, and the sangha one found at the destination was itself an argument for the teaching. Relic expositions reproduce that social fact across millennia. Queues become temporary communities. Strangers hold places for one another, share water, explain the proceedings to newcomers, carry the elderly forward. A sceptic may call this mere crowd warmth, but the tradition would answer that community formed around reverence is not a by-product of the path; the sangha is one of its three refuges. If the relic gathers the sangha, it is doing work the Buddha valued, in a form he would have recognised from his own crowded groves.

What has changed, and should be admitted plainly, is mediation. The living Buddha could correct a misunderstanding on the spot, refuse an unsuitable request, notice the person at the back. The relic cannot. That is why the tradition surrounds relics with texts, teachers and precepts — with interpretation — rather than leaving the object to speak alone. Where that surrounding discipline is strong, presence after death remains tethered to the teaching that gave it meaning. Where it weakens, presence can drift into mere aura. The difference between a shrine and a spectacle is rarely the object. It is the quality of attention the community has been taught to bring to it.


## 11. Merit, Intention and the Honest Heart

Every tradition that practises veneration eventually has to answer a blunt question from inside its own ranks: what, exactly, is supposed to happen when a person bows, offers flowers, lights a lamp or gives generously at a shrine? Buddhism's answer is among the most carefully worked out in the history of religions, and it is also among the most easily misunderstood from outside. The answer is not that the object dispenses benefits. The answer concerns the mind of the person who acts.

The key term is cetana, intention or volition. In a teaching paraphrased from the Anguttara Nikaya, the Buddha declares intention to be karma itself: having intended, a person acts by body, speech or mind, and it is the intention that gives the act its moral weight and its future fruit. A coin given with contempt and a coin given with gladness may look identical to an observer and even to the recipient's account book, but in this analysis they are different acts, because the minds that launched them were different, and minds are being shaped, moment by moment, by what they repeatedly intend. Generosity practised with joy trains joy and loosens possessiveness. Generosity performed for display trains vanity wearing a generous costume. The shrine does not confuse the two, because the shrine is not the accountant. The mind is both the actor and, over time, the result.

This framework transforms how merit, punna, should be understood. Merit is often translated in ways that make it sound like a spiritual currency — accumulated, banked, transferred, spent. There is a transactional flavour in some popular practice, and honest teachers within the tradition have criticised that flavour for centuries. But at its more careful level, the doctrine is psychological and ethical before it is anything else. A meritorious act is an act rooted in generosity, kindness and clarity rather than in greed, hatred and delusion, and such acts leave the mind more capable of happiness and less defended against truth. Their fruit is not arbitrary reward from a cosmic paymaster. It is the natural maturation of seeds deliberately planted. The gardener metaphor the texts love is exact here: one does not bribe the soil. One plants, tends, and waits, and the harvest resembles the seed.

At a reliquary, this means the tradition can be entirely sincere about the value of veneration while being entirely clear about where the value sits. It sits in the giver. The flowers will wilt by evening, and the texts know it; indeed the wilting is part of the offering's eloquence, a small daily enactment of impermanence on the shrine itself. The lamp burns down. The fruit is eaten by temple staff or given away. Nothing about this is waste, because the offering was never primarily a delivery. It was a training and a declaration: a person stood before a reminder of the awakened life and chose, with body and resources and attention, to honour awakening rather than distraction, generosity rather than clutching, reverence rather than the flat irony that protects modern people from feeling anything strongly. That choice, made deliberately, is the merit. It would remain real even if — especially if — no one was watching and nothing was received in return.

Dana, generosity, is the first of the perfections and the foundation practice for lay life in the early texts, and relic shrines have always been engines of it. The historical record of Buddhism can be read, in part, as a record of giving organised around sacred presence: stupas raised by merchants and guilds and royal women and village collectives, inscriptions at sites like Sanchi preserving the names of ordinary donors alongside great ones, monasteries fed and robed and housed by communities who understood support of the sangha as support of their own deepest aspirations. To reduce all of that to a purchase of blessings is to miss its social genius. The shrine gave scattered people a shared object of generosity, and shared generosity made a community durable. The merit was real, and so were the roads, rest houses, tanks and hospitals that pious giving so often funded along the pilgrim routes. Intention bore fruit in this world too, visibly, in brick and welfare.

The honest heart of the section title matters because the tradition also knows how easily veneration can be performed without it. The Buddha's discourses are unsparing about religious acts done for gain, fame or fear, and about teachers who encourage such motives. A gift given to secure a business advantage, a bow performed because a camera is present, an offering made in terror that misfortune will otherwise strike the family — these are not condemned as sins in a theistic sense, but they are diagnosed as weak seeds. Fear in particular is a poor gardener. It produces compliance, not freedom, and the Buddha's path aims at freedom. This is why good teachers, when they receive offerings at a relic exposition, so often redirect attention in the moment: rejoice in your giving, they will say; let the mind be glad; dedicate the merit; do not bargain. The sequence is a small liturgy of intention, purifying the act as it is performed.

Dedication of merit deserves its own word here, because it completes the logic and rescues it from any suspicion of spiritual self-enrichment. In the developed practice of both Theravada and Mahayana communities, the good of an act is deliberately given away — shared with parents and teachers, with the departed, with all beings seen and unseen. A currency that grows by being given away is not really a currency at all; the metaphor breaks, and in breaking reveals the point. Merit shared is intention widened. The person who began by honouring the Buddha ends by wishing well to strangers, rivals and creatures they will never meet. Whatever one believes about the metaphysics of transfer, the psychology is observable and the ethics are unmistakable: a heart practising universal goodwill at the shrine is being trained for universal goodwill at the office, the kitchen table and the hospital corridor.

So the honest answer to the blunt question is almost disappointingly undramatic, and therefore trustworthy. Nothing magical is supposed to happen at the reliquary. Something human is supposed to happen, and to keep happening until it becomes character: intention clarified, generosity exercised, gladness cultivated, reverence felt without embarrassment, and the whole act released, at the end, for the benefit of others. The relic's role is to occasion and focus that happening, as a portrait occasions remembrance and a grave occasions tenderness. Judged by the Buddha's own analysis of karma, that is not a small role. It is precisely the level at which human lives actually change.

## 12. The Tooth at Kandy — Relics, Kingship and the Disciples at Sanchi

If one place on earth shows how completely a relic can enter the life of a nation, it is Kandy, in the hill country of Sri Lanka, where the Sacred Tooth Relic of the Buddha rests in the temple known as the Sri Dalada Maligawa. The relic is kept in a series of nested caskets within an inner shrine room, and it is not displayed to casual view; what the visitor encounters is the casket, the drumming, the offerings, the white-clad devotees, and a rhythm of daily ritual honour that has been maintained through centuries of political upheaval, colonial occupation and civil war. The presence organises the city around it. Kandy's lake, its streets, its calendar and much of its identity take their bearings from the temple at its heart.

The Tooth's arrival in Sri Lanka is remembered by the tradition as a rescue — the relic brought from India in a time of danger, concealed, the chronicles say, in the hair of a princess — and its subsequent history is inseparable from the island's kingship. Possession of the Tooth came to signify legitimate sovereignty: the king who protected the relic was understood to hold the island in trust, and the relic's palace stood beside the king's, a permanent reminder that power was answerable to the Dhamma. When capitals moved, the Tooth moved. When invaders threatened, it was hidden. Successive rulers, including non-Buddhist colonial powers who quickly learned the political facts, found themselves obliged to reckon with it. Few objects in world history have so consistently outlasted the regimes that claimed them, quietly reversing the relationship: kings imagined they possessed the relic, while the longer story suggests the relic possessed, judged and survived the kings.

The Esala Perahera, the great annual procession centred on the Tooth, makes that relationship visible in the streets. Over a series of nights in the month of Esala, the relic casket is carried on a richly caparisoned elephant through Kandy, accompanied by dancers, drummers, fire performers, flag bearers and the processions of the island's guardian deities, whose shrines are woven into the festival's order. To watch descriptions of it is to understand that the Perahera is not a parade about a community's identity; it is one of the principal acts by which that identity is renewed. Farmers pray for rain and give thanks. Families who have moved abroad return. Children are lifted onto shoulders into an inheritance they can see and hear before they can articulate it. The political scientists call this civil religion; the devotees call it devotion; both descriptions are true, and the relic sits at the point where they meet.

A serious article should also note the questions Kandy raises, because the tradition's own scholars have raised them. When a relic becomes a palladium of the nation, whose nation is it? Sri Lanka is home to Tamil, Muslim, Christian and Hindu citizens for whom the Tooth is not an object of faith, and the island's modern history includes bitter conflict in which sacred symbols were sometimes enlisted. The best of the tradition's voices at Kandy have insisted that the relic's meaning — compassion, restraint, the impermanence of power — stands in judgement over any attempt to wield it as a banner of exclusion. A tooth of the Buddha, who taught that hatred is never appeased by hatred, cannot honestly be carried in hatred's cause. That tension is not a reason to dismiss the Kandy devotion. It is a reason to take it seriously as a living institution, with a conscience it must keep consulting.

From Kandy the mind travels naturally to Sanchi, in present-day Madhya Pradesh, where the great stupa and its carved gateways preserve some of the earliest and finest Buddhist art in India — and where a quieter relic story, involving the Buddha's two chief disciples, completes itself in modern times. Sariputta and Mahamoggallana, praised in the texts as foremost in wisdom and in meditative attainment respectively, were cremated after their deaths, which preceded the Buddha's own, and their relics were enshrined at Sanchi. There they rested for centuries, until the site's rediscovery and excavation in the nineteenth century, when the relic caskets were removed to Britain and eventually placed in a museum in London.

The return of those relics is one of the well-established and genuinely moving episodes of modern Buddhist history. After sustained effort by the Mahabodhi Society and others who argued that objects of living veneration did not belong as specimens in a distant museum, the relics of Sariputta and Mahamoggallana were brought back to India and, in 1952, ceremonially re-enshrined at Sanchi, in the new temple built near the ancient stupa. The journey home drew enormous crowds and devotion across Asia; the relics have since travelled on loan for expositions in several countries, greeted each time not as artefacts on tour but as the honoured remains of the Buddha's own disciples, teachers in their own right.

The Sanchi story clarifies something Kandy alone might leave ambiguous. Relic devotion is not only about the Buddha's body. It extends, by the tradition's own logic, to the awakened community — to the disciples whose lives proved that the path could be walked by others. Sariputta's relics do not compete with the Buddha's; they corroborate him. They say that awakening was not a solitary anomaly but a transmissible way, and that the sangha, whose relics these are, is itself worthy of gratitude. There is also a modern ethical resonance that the participants in 1952 understood clearly and that museums worldwide have debated ever since: the difference between an object and a presence is not visible under glass. It is visible in the behaviour of the people who come — in whether they come to look, or to bow, to remember, and to be instructed. Sanchi's relics were returned because enough people insisted, across a former imperial divide, that the second description was the true one.

Kingship at Kandy, nationhood tested at Kandy, discipleship honoured at Sanchi: the relics gather the largest human themes because they were entrusted, from the beginning, to communities rather than to vaults. A relic kept only safe would be a specimen. A relic kept in use — processed, offered to, argued over, brought home — remains what it was at Kushinagar when the Mallas honoured the body with dance and song and flowers: a centre around which a people remembers who it intends to be.


## 13. Relics Across the Mahayana World — Adaptation and the Corrective of Emptiness

As Buddhism travelled — along the Silk Road into Central Asia and China, by sea to Southeast Asia, across the plateau to Tibet, and onward to Korea and Japan — the relics travelled with it, and each culture that received them reshaped the devotion in its own image without, at its best, losing the centre. Tracing that adaptation is not a detour from the teaching. It is a case study in how a tradition stays alive: by letting form be refashioned while guarding, sometimes fiercely and sometimes with great humour, the insight the form exists to serve.

China received the relics as it received the texts, with imperial seriousness and a genius for enshrinement. The most famous Chinese example is the finger bone preserved at Famen Temple, northwest of Xi'an, a relic whose medieval history includes processions in which emperors welcomed it to the capital with extravagant ceremony — and at least one famous imperial critic who memorialised the throne against the whole practice, arguing in a celebrated protest that the Buddha was a foreign teacher whose bone should not be honoured at court. The controversy itself is instructive. Relic devotion in China was never uncontested background piety; it was debated at the highest level, in the language of statecraft and filial duty, and it survived the debate by becoming woven into Chinese religious life at every level, from imperial crypt to village pagoda. When the Famen Temple's underground palace was opened in the 1980s after the pagoda's partial collapse, the Tang dynasty offerings found within — silks, gold and silver vessels, and the relic caskets — offered modern scholars a sealed time capsule of that devotion, and offered modern devotees a reminder that their practice has ancestors whose craftsmanship still rebukes haste.

In Japan, relic veneration arrived with Buddhism itself in the sixth century and took on Japanese characteristics: an aesthetic of refined restraint, integration with the imperial house and later with the great temple networks, and eventually a democratisation through pilgrimage circuits and local temple treasures. Japanese Buddhism also developed, in its Zen lineages, a characteristic counter-voice — stories of masters who relativised sacred objects to jolt students out of attachment to them, a counter-voice that is itself part of the tradition's internal balance and will be considered more fully in the next section's companion discussion of excess. The point here is that adaptation in Japan did not mean dilution. It meant the relic took its place within a religious culture that also produced exquisite calligraphy of the sutras, tea offered before altars, and a discipline of attention in ordinary acts — all of them ways of saying that reverence is a quality of mind before it is a property of objects.

Tibet and the Himalayan kingdoms — Bhutan, Ladakh, the Sherpa and Tamang country of Nepal, Mustang and Dolpo — gave relic devotion yet another register, shaped by altitude, by the Vajrayana understanding of blessing, and by a landscape in which stupas, called chorten in Tibetan, mark passes, villages and thresholds. In the Himalayan world the remains of great lamas are treasured alongside relics of the Buddha and the Indian masters who carried the teaching north, and the line between relic, blessing-substance and teaching-presence is drawn differently than in the early texts. Pilgrims circumambulate chortens in high valleys where the air itself demands slowness, and the practice merges with the geography: to walk around the stupa is to walk with attention through a world understood as sacred in its structure. For communities whose historical survival depended on monasteries as schools, hospitals, treasuries and archives, relics anchored institutions that anchored everything else. Adaptation here was not decoration on a doctrine. It was the doctrine taking responsibility for a civilisation's memory.

Running through all of this diversity, the Mahayana tradition carries within itself a famous and deliberate corrective, and an honest account of relic devotion must give it its full weight. The Heart Sutra, the most beloved short text of the Mahayana world, is framed as a teaching in which the bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara, practising the perfection of wisdom, sees that the five aggregates — form, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness — are empty of independent existence, and is thereby freed from suffering. The text addresses its teaching to Sariputra, the very disciple whose relics rest at Sanchi, and its best-known line, in paraphrase, declares that form is emptiness and emptiness itself is form, and that the same is true of feeling, perception, formations and consciousness. Nothing in the aggregates stands apart, self-existing and graspable. Everything arises in dependence, and dependence, seen clearly, is not a loss of meaning but the end of the delusion that meaning requires possession.

Handled carelessly, that teaching could be wielded against relic devotion like a club: if form is emptiness, why bow to a bone? The tradition, to its credit, has generally handled it more carefully, and the care is the lesson. Emptiness in Mahayana thought does not mean that things are nothing or that distinctions are fraudulent. It means that things do not exist in the sealed, independent way the grasping mind imagines. A relic is empty in exactly this sense: it has no power sealed inside it, no essence separable from the web of memory, teaching, intention and community in which it functions. And precisely because it is empty, it can function — as a mirror functions because it is not the face, as a word functions because it is not the thing it names. The Heart Sutra does not abolish the shrine. It explains, from within the same tradition that built the shrines, why the shrine must never be mistaken for a container of magic. Sariputra is the right addressee for that explanation, and there is a deep fitness in the fact that the disciple addressed by the emptiness teaching is honoured, bones and all, at Sanchi. The tradition holds both without embarrassment: venerate the remains, and know what remains are.

This internal corrective is, in the end, the strongest evidence that relic devotion at its best is not a failure of Buddhist philosophy but one of its expressions. A tradition that chants the Heart Sutra daily in the same temples that house its relics is not a tradition that has forgotten its founder's analysis of clinging. It is a tradition practising a difficult double discipline: full reverence, empty hands. The hands fold, the head bows, the offerings are made with complete sincerity — and nothing is grasped. Form is honoured as form. Emptiness is honoured as the truth of form. The reliquary, read through the Heart Sutra, becomes a teaching device of unusual precision: the most treasured physical object in the building exists to point beyond physical objects, and it can only point because it is treasured by people who have been taught where the pointing leads.

## 14. Pilgrimage — The Road, the Four Great Places, and the Sacred Coming to the People

A relic gives devotion an address, and an address gives rise, sooner or later, to a road. Pilgrimage is what happens when reverence accepts the inconvenience of distance, and the Buddha himself, according to the tradition's own account of his final days, set the pattern with unusual directness. In the Mahaparinibbana Sutta, as Ananda raises the question of how the Buddha's followers should conduct themselves after his passing, the teaching given in paraphrase commends four places as worthy of pilgrimage for the faithful: the place of the Buddha's birth at Lumbini, the place of his awakening at Bodh Gaya, the place where he set the wheel of Dhamma turning at Sarnath, and the place of his final passing at Kushinagar. To visit these places with a devout heart, the discourse says, is to be stirred — the texts use the idea of samvega, a wholesome urgency, a shaking of the spirit that is part grief, part awe and part resolve. The founder who warned against clinging to his person nonetheless directed his followers to geography. He understood what places do to memory, and he trusted the stirring more than he feared the sentimentality.

Each of the four places stirs differently, and pilgrims have always known it. Lumbini, in the Nepalese Terai, is the quietest in its teaching: a garden, a pond, the pillar raised by Ashoka marking the spot, and the simple astonishment that everything began with a birth as vulnerable as any other. Bodh Gaya is the centre of gravity of the Buddhist world, where the Mahabodhi Temple rises beside the descendant of the tree under which the Buddha resolved not to rise until he understood, and where on any given day the chanting of a dozen nations overlaps in the precincts — Tibetan prostrations on the boards, Sri Lankan offerings at the railings, Thai, Burmese, Japanese, Vietnamese and Western voices in rotation, a living demonstration that the awakening is claimed by no single culture. Sarnath is the teaching place, deer park and ruined stupas, where the first discourse turned doctrine into a community; its mood is intellectual gratitude, the relief of a message actually delivered. Kushinagar is the place pilgrims often find hardest and, afterwards, most valuable: the reclining image, the stupa of the cremation, and the unavoidable rehearsal of one's own losses in the presence of his. A pilgrim who completes the four has walked the arc of a whole life — birth, awakening, work, death — and has had their own life quietly measured against it at every stop.

What pilgrimage does to a person is a subject the tradition analyses with the same psychological honesty it brings to meditation. First, it costs. Money, time, comfort, the small dignities of routine — pilgrimage in India has never been luxurious for most who undertake it, and the cost is not incidental to the effect. What is given up for the sake of a sacred purpose is dana practised with the whole life rather than with the wallet alone. Second, it dislodges. At home, a person's faith can become entirely conceptual, a set of opinions held in familiar surroundings. On the road, faith becomes bodily: feet, weather, queues, strangers, fatigue, food unlike home, devotion unlike home. The pilgrim discovers whether their reverence survives inconvenience, and discovers also the devotion of others — often poorer, often less educated in doctrine, often putting the visitor's refined spirituality to shame with its simplicity. Third, it equalises. Before the Mahabodhi shrine, the executive and the farmer from Bihar perform the same prostration, and the road is one of the few institutions left that routinely produces such scenes without embarrassment. Fourth, and most quietly, pilgrimage ends. The pilgrim goes home. The tradition is wise about this too: the journey's value is tested not at the shrine but in the months after, in whether the samvega harvested on the road is spent on practice or merely on nostalgia and photographs.

Against this ancient pattern, the exposition of the Buddha's relics in Arunachal Pradesh, with which this article began, appears as something both new and deeply traditional: pilgrimage reversed. Instead of the people travelling the long road to the sacred, the sacred — under guard, with ceremony, through the logistical labour of governments and monastic communities — travels the road to the people. For elderly devotees in Himalayan districts who could never have managed the journey to Bodh Gaya or to the relic shrines of Sri Lanka or Myanmar, for the sick, for the poor, for children in remote valleys, the reversal is not a dilution of pilgrimage but its democratisation. The road's hardships are borne by the organisers on the pilgrims' behalf. What remains for the people is the essential act the road was always meant to produce: the encounter, the stirring, the resolve.

Something is admittedly changed in the reversal, and honesty requires naming it. The travelling relic arrives as an event, with dignitaries and cameras and schedules, and the pilgrim's slow preparation — the saving, the planning, the weeks of approach — is compressed into a day. But something is also revealed that the fixed shrine cannot show: the sight of a whole region turning out, villages emptying toward the exposition ground, old people carried by grandchildren, monks and laypeople and officials and schoolchildren in one crowd, demonstrates to the people themselves the size and reality of their own community of faith. In regions where Buddhist communities can feel peripheral to the nation's attention, the relic's journey carries an unmistakable message that is political only in the deepest sense: your devotion is seen; your inheritance is honoured; the centre has come to the margin because, in the Dhamma's geography, the margin was never marginal.

The road and the reversal belong together, then, as the two movements of one devotion across twenty-five centuries. Ashoka's redistribution of the relics was the first great reversal — the sacred deliberately multiplied and sent outward so that no region need feel distant from it. Every exposition since, including this one in the eastern Himalaya, repeats Ashoka's logic with modern transport. And every pilgrim who still sets out for Lumbini or Kushinagar repeats the older movement, the feet answering what the heart has heard. Between the coming and the going, between the relic that travels and the pilgrim who travels, the same transaction occurs that occurred when villagers first walked to a grove outside Rajagaha to hear a living teacher: a person closes a distance on purpose, and arrives changed by having closed it.


## 15. When Devotion Goes Astray — Fraud, Commerce, and the Kalama Counsel

An article that praised relic devotion for eight sections without examining its failures would not be devotional writing at all; it would be advertising. The tradition itself has never been so naive, and the most respectful thing a writer can do for a living faith is to repeat, plainly, the criticisms it has levelled at itself. Relic devotion has gone astray in documented ways — through fraud, through commerce and through the slow slide of faith into superstition — and Buddhism's distinction is not that it avoided these corruptions but that its own founding texts supply the instruments for detecting them.

Fraud first, because it is the simplest. Wherever relics confer prestige and attract offerings, forged relics follow, as predictably as counterfeit currency follows real money. Buddhist history, like the relic history of medieval Christianity with which it invites comparison, contains episodes of rival claims to the same relic, of remains that multiplied beyond anatomical possibility, and of shrines whose treasures did not survive scrutiny. The multiplication problem was sometimes innocent — the tradition's own accounts of relics dividing and redistributing under Ashoka taught people to expect abundance — and sometimes it was not innocent at all. A relic that arrives with a merchant rather than a lineage, that cannot say where it has been for two thousand years, that appears precisely when a temple needs funds or a politician needs a ceremony, deserves the questions any historian would ask, and Buddhist scholars in every era have asked them. Provenance, inscription, material analysis and continuity of custody are not enemies of faith. They are the same honesty the Buddha demanded in doctrine, applied to objects.

Commerce is the second and more pervasive danger, because unlike fraud it rarely announces itself as corruption. It begins reasonably: pilgrims need food, lodging, transport and souvenirs; shrines need maintenance; festivals need funding. It continues, still reasonably, into an economy. It goes astray at the point where the economy stops serving the devotion and the devotion starts serving the economy — where access to the sacred is quietly rationed by price, where the best positions before the reliquary belong to donors in the way the best seats belong to sponsors, where a teacher's endorsement of a relic, a blessed object or a protective charm becomes a product line. The Buddha's own biography contains the tradition's standing rebuke to this drift: he refused to make the Dhamma a trade, taught without a fee, and in the monastic code he built, the handling of money by monks is hedged with restrictions precisely because he understood money's solvent effect on spiritual authority. A shrine economy that would have embarrassed its founder has failed a test simpler than any doctrinal one.

Superstition, the third danger, is subtler than fraud or commerce and therefore the one the texts address most carefully. The line between faith and superstition is not the line between believing and doubting; it is the line between confidence that leads to practice and credulity that replaces practice. Faith, in the Buddhist sense of saddha, is a trust that matures into investigation — one trusts the teacher enough to test the teaching in one's own life, and the trust deepens as the testing succeeds. Superstition runs the other way: it treats the sacred object as a mechanism that operates regardless of the user's life, a charm against illness, examination failure, accident or loss, valuable exactly in proportion as it demands nothing. The relic approached superstitiously is asked to do for the devotee what only the devotee's own ethical and meditative work can do. That is not devotion intensified. It is devotion hollowed out, and every serious teacher in the tradition, from the early discourses onward, has said so.

The most famous instrument the Buddha left for this whole territory is the counsel given to the Kalamas, a people confused by competing teachers each demanding allegiance, preserved in the Kalama Sutta of the Anguttara Nikaya. Its advice, in paraphrase, is to refuse belief on the grounds of tradition, hearsay, scripture, mere logic or a teacher's charisma alone, and instead to test teachings in lived experience: when you know for yourselves that certain qualities are unwholesome and lead to harm, abandon them; when you know that others are wholesome and lead to benefit, undertake them and live by them. Applied to relic devotion, the Kalama counsel cuts cleanly in both directions, which is why it remains so valuable. It forbids the cynic's shortcut as firmly as the credulous one: a person may not dismiss veneration merely because it is ancient, or popular, or uncongenial to modern taste, if their own honest experience shows it producing generosity, calm and ethical seriousness. And it forbids the devotee's shortcut with equal firmness: no relic, however authenticated, and no exposition, however moving, licenses a belief or a practice that one's own experience and conscience show to be harmful, grasping or false.

The tradition polices its excesses through more than texts. It polices them through teachers who refuse donations with strings, through monastic codes that punish the display of supposed powers and the fraudulent claim of attainments, through satire — the folk literatures of Buddhist Asia are full of greedy monks and credulous donors drawn with affectionate cruelty — and through periodic reform movements that strip accretions away and call communities back to meditation and ethics. Even the Zen counter-voice mentioned earlier, with its shocking stories of sacred images treated irreverently by masters, functions within the household as a licensed corrective: a reminder, delivered as theatre, that the object was never the point. A tradition this self-suspicious is not well described as a relic cult. It is better described as a path that has carried relics for a very long time while arguing, in every generation, about how to carry them without being carried away.

For the ordinary devotee at a modern exposition, the practical discernment is not complicated, and it can be stated as a handful of questions the tradition itself would endorse. Does this devotion make me more generous or more grasping? More honest or more evasive? Does it send me toward the sick and the suffering, as the Buddha's own presence did, or only toward further spiritual consumption? Am I being asked for money in ways that purchase spiritual rank? Am I being promised outcomes — health, success, protection — that no teacher has the right to promise? Would the practice survive the Kalama test in my own experience over the coming months? A relic exposition that passes those questions has nothing to fear from scrutiny. One that fails them has been identified, by the Buddha's own criteria, as something other than his teaching wearing his relics as costume. The distinction matters, and the tradition, at its honest best, has always insisted on making it.

## 16. Gratitude as a Practice — Repaying the Teacher with Practice

Every account of veneration should end where this one now ends: not with the object, not with the crowd, not even with the history, but with the feeling that set the whole long story in motion and the practice into which that feeling ripens when it is well taught. The Pali word is katannuta — gratitude, literally the recognition of what has been done for one. In the Buddha's teaching, gratitude is not a decorative virtue. It is treated as a mark of the integrity of a person, the quality by which one acknowledges that one's life is not self-made, and the early discourses place the person who lacks gratitude among those in whom other virtues struggle to grow. We arrive, all of us, carried: by parents, by teachers, by the labour of strangers, by a tradition that preserved texts through centuries when preservation was dangerous and expensive. The relic, at the end of the analysis, is simply the most concentrated available occasion for remembering that we were carried — and for deciding how to respond.

The Buddha was unusually explicit about how a teacher is to be repaid, and the instruction completes the argument of this entire article. Material honour has its place; he accepted monasteries, robes, medicine and meals, and praised the generosity that offered them. But when the question is what truly honours the Tathagata, the Mahaparinibbana Sutta's account of his final days gives, in paraphrase, a clear priority: the monk, nun or lay follower who lives in accordance with the Dhamma, who practises diligently and properly, is the one who honours him most highly. Flowers fade, and he is depicted as saying so, in effect, beside his own deathbed, with the sal trees showering blossoms out of season around him — a beautiful scene the text itself immediately subordinates to practice. The tradition could hardly have arranged a clearer charter for its relics. At the very moment when the body that would become the relics lay dying, the teaching declared that the highest veneration of that body was not veneration of the body. It was a life changed.

This is why gratitude, in mature Buddhist practice, so rarely terminates in an offering, however lovely the offering is. It passes through the offering into resolve. The devotee who bows at the reliquary with katannuta fully awake is not merely thanking the Buddha for teachings received, in the way one thanks an author for a book. The gratitude has a specific structure: a benefit has been received that cannot be returned to its giver, who is gone; it can only be returned forward, to others, and inward, as transformation. Parents present the same structure, which is why the texts so often pair gratitude to parents with gratitude to teachers, and why the repayment they commend in both cases is not payment but becoming: to live in a way that makes the gift fruitful. A relic exposition, understood this way, is a vast organised reminder of an unpayable debt, and the mood it produces at its best is not the sadness of debt but the energy of it — the particular joy of people who know exactly what they have been given and have decided, for an hour or a season or a life, to be worthy of it.

The dedication of merit, with which practitioners close an act of veneration, is gratitude's natural completion and the right note on which to leave the reliquary. The good of the act — the generosity, the reverence, the moment of genuine clarity before the casket — is not hoarded as a private spiritual possession. It is formally wished outward: to parents and ancestors, to teachers, to the departed whose names the family still speaks, to beings in difficulty whom the devotee will never meet, and finally, in the widest dedication, to all beings without exception. Skeptics sometimes ask what such a dedication can possibly accomplish beyond the shrine room. The tradition's answer is consistent with everything said in these sections about intention. A mind that has practised, deliberately and repeatedly, the wish that all beings share in whatever good it has done is a mind being trained out of the narrow accounting of self. Whether or not merit travels, goodwill demonstrably does — into the home that evening, into patience with a difficult relative, into the next act of generosity, which arrives more easily because the channel has been cleared by the last one. The dedication is not the decoration on the veneration. It is the veneration, arriving at its destination.

There remains, finally, the simplest description of what the people filing past the relics in Arunachal Pradesh, in Kandy, at Sanchi and at a hundred lesser-known shrines are actually doing, beneath the theology and the history and the necessary cautions. They are saying thank you to a teacher. Twenty-five centuries after his death, in languages he never spoke, in lands he never walked, people stand before the small remainder of his body and feel the most human of responses to an immeasurable gift: the wish to honour the giver, and the fear that no honour is adequate. The tradition he founded receives that wish with flowers and gold and processions, and then, with the gentleness of the teacher himself, hands it back transformed. Practise, it says. Be generous, be patient, be honest about impermanence, care for the sick, dedicate the merit, let gratitude become a life rather than a ceremony. That handing back is not a disappointment of devotion. It is devotion's graduation.

The relics will be returned to their shrine when the exposition ends. The caskets will close, the crowds will disperse to their villages and towns, the photographs will fade in importance, and the gold will wait in the dark for the next occasion. What remains in circulation is what has always remained: a teaching, a memory, a community, and — in those in whom the day's stirring took root — a slightly altered person, a little more aware that this body too is borrowed, a little more determined to spend it well. The Buddha asked for no more than that, and described it, in the teaching on honouring him, as everything. The fragment rests. The gratitude walks home on a thousand roads, and does the work.

## 17. A Dialogue with Vedanta: Presence in Form

A reader formed by Vedanta will feel, standing before a reliquary, a recognition that arrives before any argument does. The hands fold of themselves. The head bows a little. Something in the training of years — of entering a temple, of waiting for a curtain to be drawn back, of receiving a flower that has rested at a deity's feet — knows this language without needing it translated. That recognition deserves to be honoured, and it also deserves to be examined, because the dialogue between Buddhism and Vedanta at the reliquary is fruitful precisely where the two traditions are not saying the same thing.

Hindu practice has long held that presence can be honoured in form without form being mistaken for a prison. A murti is not, in thoughtful practice, a piece of stone or bronze that devotees have agreed to pretend about. It is a support for attention, a place where the formless is approached through form because human beings live through eyes and hands and knees that bend. The consecration known as prana-pratishtha is spoken of in the tradition as the invocation of living presence into the image, after which the image is treated not as a symbol only but as a host — bathed, dressed, fed, put to rest, awakened with song. Whatever metaphysical account a particular school gives of that presence, the practical effect is unmistakable. The devotee behaves toward the image as toward someone, not something. Care becomes personal. Negligence feels like discourtesy, not merely untidiness.

At the heart of that behaviour lies darshan, a word that resists a flat rendering because it names a two-way event. The devotee sees the deity, and in the same act is seen by the deity. Temple queues in India, which outsiders sometimes read as mere waiting for a glimpse, are better understood as waiting to come under a gaze. People emerge from a brief moment before the sanctum with wet eyes not because they have inspected an object but because they feel they have been met. Presence, in this grammar, is relational. It happens between.

Bring that grammar to a Buddhist reliquary and much becomes intelligible at once. The queue at a relic exposition moves in the same unhurried recognition. No one is queuing to inspect bone as a specimen. They are queuing to come near, to be for a moment in the company of what the Teacher's life left behind, and to let that nearness search them. The stupa, like the temple, organises bodies into reverence — approach, circumambulation, bowing, offering, withdrawal — and the organisation itself teaches. A person who has walked slowly around a shrine three times with folded hands has learned something in the calves and the breath that no pamphlet could have delivered.

Sri Ramakrishna stands in this dialogue as a living bridge, and he should be allowed to stand there without being made into an argument. The Hindu tradition in which he was formed counts the Buddha among the avatars of Vishnu, a reckoning far older than Ramakrishna himself, and Ramakrishna received the Buddha within that reckoning with evident love. His reverence was not the distant respect of a man praising a founder of another system. It had warmth in it, and familiarity, the tone of someone greeting a member of his own household who had taken a sterner road. At the same time, Ramakrishna's life is well known for its extraordinary experiments in other paths. He practised Islamic disciplines under a teacher and lived for a period as a Muslim devotee, and he practised Christian devotion with a surrender that moved those who watched him. In each case the pattern was the same. He did not study the path from outside and pronounce upon it. He entered it, kept its practices, and let it do its work in him, and only then spoke of what he had found.

That pattern matters for how this article holds the dialogue. Ramakrishna's example does not license conflation. He did not conclude that all paths are identical in their claims, and his reverence for the Buddha did not make him careless about what the Buddha actually taught. What his life demonstrates is a method — reverence by participation, judgment postponed until after practice, and a refusal to let doctrinal difference become an excuse for coldness. A Vedantin who follows that method at a reliquary will bow without embarrassment and without confusion. The bow does not declare that a relic is a murti. It declares that nearness to a holy life is real, that gratitude is owed, and that the body may say so.

There is also a caution travelling in the opposite direction, and honest dialogue requires naming it. Vedantic devotion, at its most tender, speaks of the divine taking form out of compassion for devotees who need a face and a name. Buddhist devotion at the reliquary arises from a different soil. The relic is honoured not because the Buddha chose to inhabit it but because the Buddha is gone, and gone in a way the tradition refuses to soften. The parinirvana is final. What remains is remains. To read the reliquary through the murti alone would be to miss the ache that gives Buddhist relic devotion its distinctive gravity, the ache of a presence that is honoured precisely as a trace of absence. The two grammars rhyme. They do not coincide, and the rhyme is more beautiful, and more instructive, when the difference is kept audible.

So the dialogue begins not with agreement but with shared posture. Two traditions kneel in the same direction for reasons that overlap and diverge. The Vedantin brings a refined understanding of how form can mediate presence and how being seen transforms the seer. The Buddhist brings a relic that will not let presence be separated from loss. Between them, a devotee at the Golden Pagoda may find that both lessons are needed on the same morning — how to come close, and how to let go of the very thing one has come close to. That double lesson is the subject of the section that follows.

## 18. What Vedanta Learns from the Reliquary, and What Buddhism Asks Back

Every genuine dialogue changes both speakers, and it changes them most where each is most certain. For Vedanta, the reliquary presses on the question of impermanence with a weight that arguments alone rarely achieve. For Buddhism, the long Vedantic reflection on devotion presses back with a question about warmth, continuity, and the knower who remains when all else passes. Neither question is a refutation. Both are gifts, if they are received as questions and not rushed into settlements.

Begin with what Vedanta learns. The central affirmation of Advaita Vedanta, in the teaching associated with Shankara and carried forward by generations of teachers, is that the Atman, the Self, is not born and does not die, that it is the changeless witness beneath the changing play of body and mind. Against that affirmation the Buddhist teaching of anatta is often set, and rightly, as its great counterpoint. The Buddha's analysis, as the early discourses present it, finds no permanent, independent self among the aggregates of body, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness, and declines to build liberation on a metaphysical substance that cannot be found in experience. This is a real difference. It is not a misunderstanding that better translation will dissolve, and this article will not pretend otherwise. A tradition that affirms the eternal Self and a tradition that examines experience and finds no such self to point to are not using different words for one doctrine.

And yet the reliquary teaches something that sits beneath the doctrinal dispute and addresses the devotee rather than the debater. Stand before bone that was once part of a living teacher who ate, walked, grew old, fell ill, and died, and the truth that all compounded things decay stops being a proposition. Vedanta knows this truth in its own idiom. Its teachers have always insisted that the body is perishable, that name and form pass, that clinging to the transient as though it were permanent is the root of sorrow. The discrimination between the eternal and the non-eternal stands near the very beginning of the Vedantic path. But knowing a truth as the first item in a list of qualifications is one thing, and kneeling before its evidence is another. The reliquary gives Vedantic detachment a face and a date. It says, in effect, that even the most awakened life the world has known was not exempt, and that the exemption of the Self was never meant to be a shelter from grief at the passing of forms. A Vedantin who has wept honestly at a reliquary may return to the doctrine of the changeless Atman with cleaner hands, less tempted to use changelessness as an anaesthetic.

Buddhism, in return, asks Vedanta a searching question about devotion. If the Self is ever-present and nearer than nearness, why does the heart still need the queue, the shrine, the relic, the image? Vedantic practice has its own rich answers — that the mind learns through form, that love requires an object until it ripens into recognition, that the Lord's condescension to form is grace. The Buddhist question does not overturn those answers. It sharpens them. It asks whether the form is being used as a raft or hoarded as property, whether darshan ends in gratitude and release or in a subtler grasping that has learned religious vocabulary. The reliquary is a severe teacher here because its object visibly refuses to be possessed. The relics will leave Namsai when the exposition ends. They were never the devotee's to keep. Devotion that has passed through that refusal is devotion purified of ownership, and Vedanta, which aims at a freedom beyond all possession, can recognise a friend in that severity.

The question Buddhism receives in return is equally searching. A path that analyses the self so rigorously, and that warns so faithfully against clinging, must still account for the tears of the pilgrim at the shrine, and account for them without embarrassment. Buddhist tradition in fact does so, in its doctrines of recollection, gratitude, and merit, and in centuries of poetry and pilgrimage that no one could mistake for coldness. But the Vedantic presence in the dialogue keeps the question warm. It asks whether knowledge, in becoming precise, risks becoming thin — whether a practitioner can be correct about impermanence and yet unpractised in love. The mutual correction that emerges from the whole exchange can be stated plainly. Buddhism corrects devotion that drifts toward attachment, reminding every tradition that the holiest object must finally be released. Vedanta corrects knowledge that drifts toward coldness, reminding every tradition that analysis was undertaken for the sake of liberation, and liberation, in a human life, has a warm face or it has not yet been tasted.

There is a further fruitfulness in leaving the central dispute unsettled, and it is worth saying why, since modern religious writing often treats unresolved difference as failure. When the conversation between anatta and Atman is allowed to remain a conversation, practitioners on both sides are kept honest in a way that victory would spoil. The Vedantin who must regularly face the Buddhist examination of experience is less likely to turn the Self into a concept comfortably possessed. The Buddhist who must regularly face the Vedantic witness is less likely to turn no-self into a slogan comfortably recited. Each doctrine is protected by the other's questions from becoming a possession of the ego it was meant to dissolve. That may be the deepest service the reliquary performs for Vedanta, and the temple performs for Buddhism — not agreement, but a standing invitation to be examined by a friend.

The exposition at Namsai, then, is not only a Buddhist event at which Hindus are welcome. It is a small, enacted chapter in one of the oldest conversations in Indian religious life, a conversation that began when Buddhist and Brahmanical teachers first disputed in groves and courts and has continued, in monasteries and maths and printed pages, ever since. The queue moves slowly. The conversation moves slowly too, across centuries, and a pilgrim who joins either queue joins both.


## 19. The Relics of the Saints in Other Houses

The impulse to keep and honour what a holy life has left behind is not the property of one religion, and a brief journey through neighbouring houses can deepen a pilgrim's reverence for the Buddhist reliquary by showing how differently the same human longing has been housed. The comparisons that follow are offered in a spirit of respect for each tradition in its own integrity. Resemblance at the level of practice does not mean identity at the level of meaning, and each house must be allowed to explain its own treasures in its own words.

In the Christian churches, especially in the Catholic and Orthodox traditions, the relics of the saints have been venerated for many centuries. The saints are honoured as witnesses — men and women in whom, as the tradition teaches, grace bore visible fruit — and what remains of their bodies, or objects closely associated with their lives, has been kept in altars, carried in procession, and visited by pilgrims seeking intercession and encouragement. The theology that surrounds this practice is careful in its own way. Honour shown to a relic is directed, in the classic teaching, beyond the object to the saint, and beyond the saint to God, whose work the saint's life displays. A pilgrim at the tomb of a beloved saint is not, in the tradition's self-understanding, worshipping bone. The pilgrim is keeping company with a witness whose life still instructs, and asking to be remembered in that witness's prayers. A Buddhist pilgrim will recognise the structure — honour passing through an object toward a life, and through a life toward a truth — while also noticing the difference. The Christian saint is alive with God and intercedes; the Buddha, in the early Buddhist understanding, has passed beyond reach, and the relic works not by intercession but by recollection and inspiration. The practices look like cousins. Their inner logics are distinct, and both traditions are better served by noticing that than by blurring it.

The Sufi dargah offers another neighbouring house, and in India it is often literally a neighbour, standing in the same towns and sometimes drawing some of the same families who visit temples and gurdwaras. At the tomb of a Sufi saint, visitors come with flowers, with cloth offerings, with petitions and with thanksgiving for petitions answered. Music may fill the courtyard in the evening. Food is distributed. The atmosphere at a great dargah on a festival night — crowds, song, the press of the hopeful and the grateful — teaches an observer that the tomb functions as a court of a beloved friend of God, a place where the saint's baraka, his spiritual blessing-power, is sought by people who feel themselves in need of a mediator of mercy. Historians and theologians within Islam have debated tomb veneration across the centuries, and that internal debate belongs to Islam itself. What the visitor from another tradition can honour without trespassing is the evident human reality: generations have found at these tombs a place where grief could be spoken aloud and hope could borrow courage from a life remembered as close to God.

The Sikh tradition presents a different and especially instructive form of presence. After the line of human Gurus, Guru Gobind Singh conferred the Guruship, as Sikh teaching holds, upon the Guru Granth Sahib, the community's sacred scripture, which is therefore treated not as a book about presence but as the living Guru's presence itself. The Guru Granth Sahib is awakened in the morning and put to rest at night, fanned with a whisk, carried under a canopy, installed on a raised seat, and attended with a protocol of honour that any visitor to a gurdwara can observe. No relic of a body is involved, and that is precisely the instruction. Presence here is carried by the Word — by hymns of the Gurus and of saints included in the volume — and the community's reverence is directed to the teaching enthroned among them. A Buddhist reader may think at once of the Buddha's own parting indication that the Dhamma would be the teacher after his passing, a parallel explored more fully later in this article. The Sikh example shows that indication embodied in daily protocol: a community that quite literally makes room, every morning, for the teaching to take its seat.

What does this brief tour give the pilgrim returning to the reliquary at Namsai? First, humility about uniqueness. No tradition should claim to have invented gratitude toward the remains of the holy. Second, clarity about distinctness. Intercession, blessing-power, the enthroned Word, and recollection of a teacher who has passed utterly beyond — these are four different accounts of why a shrine matters, and flattening them into a single vague spirituality would insult all four. Third, and most practically, a widened sympathy. A pilgrim who has understood why a Christian grandmother lights a candle at a saint's tomb, why a family spreads a cloth at a dargah, and why a Sikh attendant fans the Guru Granth Sahib will approach the Buddhist queue with a heart already trained. The training is the same in every house: slow down, behave as a guest, let other people's love for what they honour instruct your own.

There is one more lesson, and it concerns India itself, where these houses have stood within walking distance of one another for centuries and where the exposition at Namsai is now drawing its queues. Indian religious life at its best has rarely been a sealed system. It has been a conversation conducted in shared streets, shared festivals, shared vocabularies of offering and gratitude, even amid real theological disagreement and, at times, real conflict that should not be romanticised away. The relics travelling to Arunachal Pradesh enter that old conversation as honoured guests. They will be received by Buddhists with the devotion of their own tradition, and they will be witnessed by neighbours of other traditions with the recognition this section has tried to describe. Both the devotion and the recognition are forms of the same civic virtue — the ability to stand reverently in a house that is not one's own.

## 20. Heritage, Museums and the Sacred

Sooner or later, every sacred object in the modern world meets an institution with a climate-controlled vault, a catalogue number, and a conservation laboratory. The meeting is unavoidable, and it is not in itself a misfortune. The relics of the Buddha that have come to Namsai rest, when they are not on exposition, in the custody of the National Museum in New Delhi, and that single fact contains the whole tension this section explores: an object can be, at once and without contradiction in practice, an item of national heritage requiring professional conservation and an altar at which people weep. The work of our time is to hold both truths without letting either devour the other.

Consider what the museum contributes, stated fairly. Conservation science protects fragile remains from humidity, light, pests, vibration, and the slow chemistry of decay. Documentation establishes provenance, without which sacred objects are vulnerable to theft, forgery, and the fog of competing claims. Secure custody allows objects to travel — as the relics have travelled to Namsai — under conditions that a purely local arrangement might struggle to guarantee. Scholars attached to museums produce the careful histories on which honest devotion, of the kind this article has tried to practise, depends. A devotee who knows what is actually known about Piprahwa, and what remains debated, has been served by the museum's discipline, not hindered by it. Gratitude is in order, and it should be specific: to conservators whose names the public rarely learns, whose work is done in gloves, in quiet rooms, on objects before which their own hearts may or may not bow, but which their hands unfailingly respect.

Consider then what devotion contributes, stated just as fairly. A sacred object in a museum is in danger of a subtle demotion — not to worthlessness, but to mere significance, the fate of being endlessly explained and never encountered. Devotion restores encounter. When relics leave the vault for exposition, when a queue forms before dawn, when an elderly pilgrim who will never read a catalogue entry presses her palms together and closes her eyes, the object resumes its full stature. It is no longer only evidence of the past. It is an occasion of transformation in the present. Museum professionals in India, to their credit, have often understood this better than theory would predict, arranging expositions, facilitating darshan, and cooperating with monastic communities, because they know the objects in their care are not like other objects. A sword in a vault is heritage. A relic in a vault is heritage that somebody's grandmother is waiting to venerate, and custody that forgets the grandmother has misunderstood its charge.

The events of 2025 gave this whole question a sharp and happy illustration. Jewels associated with the Piprahwa relics — part of the assemblage excavated in the nineteenth century and long held outside India — were returned to India in 2025 after an episode that drew wide public attention, and they were received not merely as recovered property but as a homecoming. The language of repatriation, developed for the return of cultural property taken in colonial circumstances, fits sacred objects with a special exactness. For an ordinary artefact, return restores ownership and national patrimony, and that is already a serious good. For a sacred object, return restores a relationship. The jewels that once accompanied the relics in their stupa were offerings — gifts laid down by devotees in acts of worship. Their removal severed not just a collection but a gesture. Their return allows the gesture to be completed again in the imagination of the community, and allows the objects to resume their place within a living web of reverence rather than a foreign display. What repatriation means for sacred objects generally can be put in one sentence: it acknowledges that some things are not fully described by the category of property, and that justice toward them includes restoring the company they were made to keep.

None of this requires romanticising the past or demonising the present custodians of disputed objects, and the honest devotee will resist both temptations. Excavation, preservation, and scholarship in earlier centuries were mixed enterprises, carried out by people of mixed motives, and some sacred objects survive at all because someone, however imperfectly, dug carefully and kept records. The mature position is harder and better: gratitude for preservation, insistence on return where return is due, and a refusal to let old wrongs become an excuse for new carelessness. The relics at Namsai model the synthesis. They are conserved by professionals, documented by scholars, guarded by the state, expounded by the press, and venerated by monks and laypeople — five kinds of care around one small, silent centre. When an object is both artefact and altar, it does not need its guardians to agree about everything. It needs each of them to perform their own kind of care excellently and to respect the others' kinds as real.

A final word belongs to the pilgrim who may feel, in a museum gallery, a pang of alienation before a glass case, or, at an exposition, a pang of worry that devotion is being staged. Both pangs are worth listening to and neither should be obeyed absolutely. Glass can protect without imprisoning, if the heart approaches the case as it would approach a shrine. And a well-organised exposition can serve devotion without manufacturing it, as any pilgrim who has felt a crowded queue turn suddenly silent knows. The sacred has survived worse custodians than museums, and heritage has survived worse threats than incense. At Namsai this November, artefact and altar are, for a few days, the same address, and that temporary identity is itself one of the exposition's quiet teachings: that care, in all its dialects, is a single language spoken with different accents.


## 21. If You Go to Namsai

This section is a guide, but not the kind printed on the back of a ticket. It offers no timings, no fees, no routes, and no advice about transport or lodging, because those are matters to learn from official sources and from the people organising your journey, and a devotional article that invented them would betray its subject. What it offers instead is a preparation of the heart for the public darshan of the relics at the Golden Pagoda in Namsai, where the exposition announced by the Press Information Bureau runs from 14 to 20 November 2026. The outer journey will be arranged by others and by circumstance. The inner journey is yours to arrange, and it can begin tonight, wherever you are reading this.

Begin, in the days before you travel, by letting the visit acquire a reason. Pilgrimage traditions across Asia agree on this much: the traveller who arrives with a formed intention receives more than the traveller who arrives with only curiosity, though curiosity is welcome and often ripens on the road. Sit quietly one evening and ask what you are carrying to Namsai. It may be gratitude for a teaching that steadied you in a hard year. It may be grief that has not yet found a place to be spoken. It may be a parent's memory, or a child's question, or a simple wish to stand once in the presence of what the Buddha's life left behind. Name it, inwardly and plainly. An intention named is not a demand made upon the relics. It is a direction given to your own attention, so that when the moment of darshan comes — and it will be brief, as such moments always are — your heart knows what it came to do and does not spend the moment wondering.

Prepare, too, by reading a little and by arguing less. A short account of the Buddha's final days, read slowly in the evenings before the journey, will furnish the imagination with the road to Kushinagar, the grove, the last instructions, the stillness after the passing, so that the reliquary at Namsai is seen against its true background. This is not the week for debates about doctrine, whether in books or on screens. Controversy sharpens the intellect and scatters the mood a pilgrim needs. Arrive instead with a mind that has been recently in the company of the story, the way one arrives at the house of a revered elder having recalled, on the way, the kindnesses one has received there.

On the day itself, let the queue become part of the practice rather than an obstacle before it. A queue at a great exposition is a strange and wonderful institution: hundreds of strangers, of different regions, languages, incomes, and degrees of learning, voluntarily arranged in a single slow line by nothing but shared reverence. Treat the slowness as instruction. The person ahead of you is not a delay between you and the relics. That person is a fellow pilgrim whose grandmother may have taught them the same refuges you learned from yours, or whose presence here, after a long bus journey you know nothing about, may represent a devotion that shames your own convenience. Let the queue teach patience in the body — in the feet, in the shoulders, in the breath — because patience learned in the body is available later, in traffic and in hospitals and in family quarrels, when it is actually needed. If prayers or recollections are your custom, the queue is their natural home. If silence is your custom, the queue will honour it. What the queue does not reward is the restless documentation of every minute. Photograph, where photography is permitted, as the organisers direct, and then let the device rest. A photograph taken at a shrine is a fine servant of memory. It is a poor substitute for having been there, and the moment of darshan, which lasts seconds, deserves the whole of you for those seconds.

The offering you bring should be simple enough to carry your intention without distracting from it. Flowers fade by evening, and that is their eloquence. A small offering given with a bow says what the whole exposition says: beauty is offered, not stored; gratitude is expressed, not invested. If you offer nothing material, offer the thing the tradition has always valued most, which is a resolve formed on the spot. Pilgrims in many traditions leave a shrine having quietly promised something — a reconciliation attempted, a habit surrendered, a practice begun — and the promise, kept in obscurity over the following months, becomes the relic the pilgrim takes home. The relics themselves will return to Delhi when the exposition closes. No one takes them away. But a resolve travels without a permit, weighs nothing, and cannot be confiscated by circumstance. Choose yours before you reach the front of the queue, so that the bow and the promise are a single act.

When the moment comes, do not strain for an experience. This may be the most useful sentence in the section. Pilgrims sometimes arrive at great shrines secretly negotiating for a vision, a thrill, a sign, and leave disappointed with a holiness that offered them only quiet. The relics offer quiet. A small reliquary, a bowed head, a breath that slows of itself, perhaps tears, perhaps nothing that could be named at all — and then the line moves, and the moment is over, and its work begins in the days after. Trust the smallness. Seeds are not impressive at the time of planting. If emotion comes, receive it with gratitude and do not perform it for neighbours. If dryness comes, receive that too, and do not conclude that you have failed at devotion. The Teacher whose remains rest there spent a lifetime warning against the grasping after experiences, and it would be a fine irony, and a gentle one, to be taught that lesson by his reliquary in the very act of veneration.

Afterwards, linger a little in the precincts if the arrangements allow, not to repeat the darshan but to let it settle. Watch other pilgrims emerge. Notice the faces of the very old and the very young, who tend at shrines to wear the same expression. Then turn homeward with the resolve in your pocket and the photograph, if you took one, in its proper rank — second. In the weeks that follow, the truest measure of the journey will not be what you can describe to friends. It will be whether anything in the conduct of an ordinary Tuesday has quietly changed. That is the darshan that keeps giving, long after the pagoda has disappeared from the rear window.

## 22. A Seven-Day Practice of Grateful Impermanence

For the reader who cannot travel to Namsai, and equally for the reader who has returned from it, this section sets out seven days of practice, one for each day of a single week, each complete in itself and each turning the article's themes into something the hands and hours can do. The practices are deliberately modest. None requires equipment, expense, or unusual leisure. What they require is the one offering the Buddha's teaching always asks for and the modern schedule always resists: unhurried attention, given on purpose, to things that are passing.

The first day belongs to recollection of the Buddha. Set aside a period in the morning, before the day's noise has organised you, and bring the Teacher to mind not as a statue or a symbol but as a person who lived a life in time. Follow, in imagination and at a walking pace of thought, the broad arc that the tradition hands down: the sheltered youth, the encounters with sickness, age, and death, the departure at night, the years of striving, the night of awakening, the decades of walking and teaching that followed, and the final illness accepted without complaint. The point of the exercise is not historical precision in every detail. It is relationship. A teacher recalled as a person can be thanked as a person, and gratitude toward a person has a warmth that agreement with a philosophy never quite achieves. End the recollection by considering one quality of that life — its patience, say, or its refusal to retaliate — and carrying that single quality, like a lamp picked up from a shrine, into the day's errands. Notice, as the day ends, how often the quality was needed. That noticing is the practice completing itself.

The second day is given to mortality reflection, undertaken calmly and without morbidity. The Buddhist tradition has always treated the recollection of death as a tonic rather than a terror, a way of restoring proportion to a mind that spends its days treating the urgent as though it were the important. Choose a quiet quarter of an hour and consider, in plain terms, that your own life, like every life you love, is a temporary arrangement. This is not a speculation. It is the most certain fact available to you, and the practice consists simply in not looking away from it for fifteen minutes. Let the fact do its gentle work. Petty resentments shrink in its presence, because few quarrels deserve the limited currency of a mortal's afternoon. Affection grows more articulate, because people we shall not always have become, in this light, people to thank today. If fear arises, let it be there without obeying it; fear at the thought of death is usually love wearing a frightened face, love for life and for particular lives, and it softens when it is named as love. Close the reflection by doing one small thing the reflection recommends — the message sent, the appreciation spoken aloud — so that mortality has produced tenderness rather than merely thought.

The third day turns outward, to an act of nursing or service. Impermanence is not an abstraction to the sick, the elderly, and the exhausted; it is their daily weather, and the tradition's reverence for the Buddha's own attendance on a sick monk, an episode the texts preserve with pride, establishes service as a form of devotion equal to any offering of flowers. Visit someone whose world has narrowed. Sit at the speed of their afternoon rather than yours. Practical help matters — the errand run, the form filled, the meal left within reach — but presence matters more, because what confinement starves is not chiefly the body but the sense of still belonging to the common world. If no sick person is within your reach, serve where service is unglamorous and unrecorded: the colleague's tedious task quietly absorbed, the public place left cleaner than you found it. The day's discipline is to perform the service without converting it into a story about yourself. Impermanence, contemplated on the second day, becomes compassion on the third, because to see clearly that everyone is passing is to see that everyone deserves company on the way.

The fourth day practises generosity without witness, which is generosity purified of its commonest adulterant. Give something real — money that could have been spent on yourself, time that was genuinely scarce, an object you actually like — and arrange the giving so that no one knows, or so that as few know as the mechanics of the gift allow. Do not announce it, do not record it for an audience, and resist the later temptation to let it slip into conversation wearing the costume of an aside. The practice exposes, with surgical kindness, how much of ordinary giving is a transaction in which appreciation is part of the payment. Giving without witness collects no payment, and the giver discovers whether the act itself is satisfying. Buddhist teaching on giving has always prized the state of the giver's mind before, during, and after the gift, and this day trains all three: gladness in deciding, openness in releasing, and a clean forgetting afterwards. What is released without witness is also a rehearsal for the final release the whole article concerns. Everything we love will be given up unwitnessed in the end. Practising small unwitnessed givings is how the heart learns that release can be an act of joy rather than only of loss.

The fifth day takes circumambulation home. Pilgrims walk around stupas and shrines keeping the sacred object to their right, and the practice gathers the body's oldest devotional knowledge: to move slowly, in a circle, around a centre, without arriving anywhere, because the point was never arrival. Adapted honestly — and the adaptation should be honest, not pretending your house is a stupa — the practice becomes this. Choose the place in your home, or in a temple or garden you can visit, that best represents your centre: the small shrine shelf, the chair where you read what matters, the tree under which your family gathers. Walk slowly around it, or around the room or garden that contains it, for a set number of circuits, in silence, attending to the feet and the breath and the turning itself. The mind will protest that nothing is happening, which is the protest of a mind addicted to destinations. Stay with the turning. A circle is the shape of seasons, of the year, of the returning festivals, of grief and recovery, and walking it teaches the body what the doctrine of impermanence teaches the mind — that going around is not going nowhere, that recurrence with attention is itself a form of progress. End by bowing to the centre, whatever it is, in thanks for holding still while you learned to move.

The sixth day is for dedicating merit, the practice by which the good of the previous days is deliberately given away. Sit at the day's end and review the week so far without self-congratulation: the recollection, the reflection, the service, the hidden gift, the walking. Then, in whatever words your own tradition gives you or in plain words of your own, direct the goodness of it all away from yourself — toward the sick person you visited, toward the dead you still miss, toward the teachers living and long gone, toward beings you will never meet whose suffering you cannot relieve by any other means. Sceptical readers sometimes ask what such dedication accomplishes, and the honest answer begins with what it accomplishes in the dedicator. It breaks the last subtle grasping, the grasping at spiritual achievement itself. Merit hoarded becomes a possession, and possessions are what this week has been learning to release. Merit dedicated becomes a river rather than a tank. The practice also repairs the imagination's geography: for a few minutes, the welfare of distant and unknown beings is treated as a real destination for one's own small goodness, and a heart that practises that geography weekly becomes, measurably, harder to frighten into selfishness.

The seventh day is kept in silence and listening, and it is placed last because it requires the week's accumulated steadiness. Choose a stretch of the day — an hour is enough for a first attempt, a morning is better — and let speech rest. Tell those around you beforehand, briefly, so the silence reads as practice rather than mood. Without conversation to manage, the senses return like servants who had been waiting outside a closed door. Sounds arrive with their distances and textures restored. Other people's faces, uninterpreted by commentary, become legible again. Listening, in this protected quiet, reveals itself as the foundation under every other practice of the week: the recollection was listening to a life, the service was listening to a need, the dedication was listening for where goodness wanted to go. End the silence gradually, and let the first words you speak be chosen ones — thanks, ideally, to someone specific. A week that ends in chosen gratitude has enacted the article's whole argument in miniature. Everything practised was impermanent. Every day of it has already passed. And the gratitude, which is the week's relic, remains in the practitioner's keeping, to be carried into the ordinary weeks that follow and renewed there, one unremarkable, unrepeatable day at a time.


## 23. The Teacher Who Pointed Away from Himself

There is a paradox at the centre of relic veneration, and the tradition has never hidden it. The man whose remains draw the longest queues in Asia spent his teaching life redirecting attention away from his own person and toward the truth he had discovered and the practice that leads to it. He declined, as the early discourses consistently portray him, to be treated as the destination. Again and again the emphasis falls the same way: the teaching is the guide, the practice is the path, and the teacher, however loved, is the one who points. To venerate his relics without hearing that redirection would be to honour the finger and ignore everything it indicates. To hear the redirection and abandon the veneration would be to miss why the pointing hand is loved. The mature pilgrim holds both, and the holding is not difficult once its logic is seen. We thank the signpost. We do not camp beneath it and call the journey complete.

The Mahaparinibbana Sutta, the great discourse on the Buddha's final days, gathers this teaching into its most remembered form. In its closing movement, with the Teacher's passing near and the community's grief rising, the instruction given to those who would remain is that they should be islands unto themselves, or lamps unto themselves — the Pali image has carried both renderings, island and lamp, and translators have honestly differed over which the word supports, a small uncertainty this article notes rather than conceals — with no other refuge, taking the Dhamma as their island or lamp and their refuge. The setting gives the words their weight. This is not a teacher hoarding authority at the end, nor founding a cult of his own memory. It is a teacher, at the moment when dependence would be most forgivable, formally handing the community back to itself and to the teaching. The relics and the instruction belong to the same event and interpret each other. The body that would become relics is the body of the man who said, in effect, do not make a refuge of me. Veneration that remembers the sentence is safe. Veneration that forgets it becomes the very grasping he diagnosed.

It helps to be precise about what taking the Dhamma as refuge actually involves, because the phrase can sound like a retreat into books. The Dhamma in the Buddha's usage is not primarily a literature. It is the truth of how things are — that compounded things arise, endure a while, and pass; that suffering follows craving as a shadow follows a cart; that a path of conduct, meditation, and wisdom loosens craving's grip — together with the teaching that communicates that truth and the practice that verifies it. To take refuge in the Dhamma is therefore a daily, experimental act. It means testing the teaching in the laboratory of one's own griefs and habits, and trusting it in proportion as it proves itself there. A relic can start that experiment. It cannot substitute for it. The pilgrim who leaves Namsai moved, and who then meditates a little more honestly, gives a little more freely, and speaks a little more carefully in the following month, has understood the reliquary better than a scholar who leaves with perfect notes and unchanged habits.

This is also where the devotion of the earlier sections finds its justification and its limit, stated together. Justification, because human beings do not in fact begin with abstractions. We begin with persons. The child learns goodness from a grandmother before learning ethics; the student loves mathematics through a teacher before loving it purely; the pilgrim's heart is first caught by a life — by the departure at night, the striving, the compassion, the serene death — and only through that love is led to the impersonal truth the life embodied. The Buddha, who taught skilful means and adjusted his discourse to every listener, can hardly be imagined begrudging his followers their doorway. The relics are the doorway that survives him, the last piece of his skilful means, still gathering people who would never open a discourse and holding them, for one quiet moment, in the presence of what his life meant. Limit, because a doorway is for passing through. The tradition's own stories are unsparing toward devotion that settles at the threshold — toward merit collected like coin, toward shrines visited as transactions, toward a Teacher praised in superlatives by people declining his medicine. The reliquary's final question to every pilgrim is not whether you bowed, but what the bow set in motion.

Seen in this light, the whole exposition can be read as the Teacher's last sermon, preached without words by the arrangement of things themselves. A life of unmatched significance ends in a handful of remains. The remains, divided, travel, are fought over in legend, buried, excavated, catalogued, and carried at last to a golden pagoda in the hills of Arunachal Pradesh, where strangers queue in silence to look at almost nothing. Almost nothing is exactly the sermon. Everything the pilgrim expected to grasp dissolves under the gaze — no statue's grandeur, no relic's spectacle, only the small, irreducible fact of a life fully lived and fully relinquished. Those who have ears for it hear, in that smallness, the entire teaching at once: that significance does not require permanence, that love does not require possession, and that a teacher's greatness may be measured by how thoroughly he arranged to be, in the end, unnecessary — present everywhere in the practice of his students and graspable nowhere as a possession of theirs.

So honour the relic, and let the honouring complete itself in the only way the Teacher ever asked. Learn one teaching a little more deeply this season. Keep one precept a little more carefully. Sit down, in silence, a little more often than comfort prefers. The stupa has always known this secret, and its architecture preaches it to anyone who walks around it slowly enough: the relic is at the centre, hidden, unmoving, and unseen, while the pilgrim's own feet do the circling, the changing, and the learning. The centre holds still so that the circumference can move. What moves, in the end, is us.

## 24. Conclusion: What Remains

An article that began among relics should end by asking, without flinching, the question its title has carried from the start. What remains?

Bone remains, a little of it, guarded and golden-housed, travelling under escort between a museum vault in Delhi and a pagoda in Namsai, and that little is more eloquent than monuments. Empires that tried to outlast time in stone are studied now as ruins, while the remains of a wandering teacher who owned a bowl and a robe are received, twenty-five centuries later, with folded hands by people whose languages he never heard. The contrast is not a trick of survival. It is a verdict on what human beings finally value. Power impresses for a generation and embarrasses for ten. A life that demonstrated, in full public view, how to hold everything loosely and love everyone steadily does not date. It waits, in its small reliquary, for each new century to discover that it needs exactly that demonstration.

The Dhamma remains, and it remains in the only way truth ever does — not in vaults, though vaults have their use, but in practice, renewed or neglected in every generation. The teachings survived the Teacher because monks memorised them, because councils recited them, because scribes copied them onto leaves that themselves decayed and were copied again, because translators carried them across languages at the cost of lifetimes, and because, in every age, ordinary practitioners sat down and tested them against their own suffering and found them good. That chain of custody is longer and more fragile than any museum's, and it runs through the reader's own hands now. What remains of the Buddha in your neighbourhood, in your household, in the temper of your speech under provocation, is precisely as much Dhamma as you practise, and not one discourse more. The exposition will be reported, photographed, and filed. The chain of custody will be extended, or not, at kitchen tables and in hospital corridors and beside deathbeds, in the unrecorded places where the teaching has always actually lived.

Presence and absence remain, held together, which is perhaps the article's deepest thread. The pilgrim at the Golden Pagoda stands before a presence that is made entirely of absence — that is the reliquary's grammar, learned across these sections from the division of the ashes to the queue in the November light. And the lesson generalises, gently, over a whole life. Every beloved face we know is becoming a memory even while it smiles at us. Every house we have loved will be, or already is, someone else's house. The traditions gathered in this article — Buddhist, Vedantic, Christian, Sufi, Sikh — differ in nearly everything else, but they converge in refusing to call this condition a defect. Absence, met honestly, becomes the school of gratitude. Presence, received as temporary, becomes luminous rather than merely pleasant. The relic is simply the most concentrated form of a curriculum the whole world is teaching all the time, to students who mostly arrive, as we all do, a little late and entirely forgiven.

On 21 November 2026 the exposition at Namsai will end. The reliquary will be closed with ceremony, the escort will form, and the relics will begin their return to Delhi, to the quiet and the climate control and the catalogue's calm. The Golden Pagoda will resume its ordinary beauty. Buses will carry pilgrims down out of the hills, and kitchens in a hundred towns will hear the journey retold over ordinary meals, the details already softening, as details do. None of that is failure or anticlimax. It is the teaching, enacting itself one final time at the level of the event: the revered thing departs, on schedule, in full view, and no one who understood it reaches out to hold the convoy back. What the relics awakened stays behind, because it was never in the reliquary. It is in the resolve formed in the queue, in the seven days of practice waiting on a kitchen calendar, in the phone call finally made to the sick friend, in the gift no one witnessed, in the silence kept on the seventh morning. Those are the relics of the exposition, distributed without division, like the original ashes, into more households than any procession could visit.

What remains, then, is what the Buddha always said would remain, and what Sri Ramakrishna, bowing to him across the centuries, would have recognised with a smile: a teaching that works, a gratitude that steadies, and a road that continues under the feet of whoever chooses, this week, to walk it. The pagoda empties. The road fills. Bow once more, in whatever direction your shrine faces, and go home grateful — for the bone, for the absence, for the brief November days when the two were one address, and for everything in your own passing, precious life that their meeting taught you to hold with open hands.
